## Quantum Fokker-Planck solvers as trainable generative models 

Tariq Mahassen, Marcin Jastrzebski, and Oleksandr Kyriienko 

School of Mathematical and Physical Sciences, University of Sheffield, Sheffield S3 7RH, United Kingdom 

We develop and analyze generative models based on quantum solvers of stochastic differential equations. Unlike the common approach based on layered variational quantum circuits with pa-rameterized rotations, we parameterize the potential of a Langevin process. The associated Fokker-Planck (FP) equations are Hermitianized and solved using a linear combination of cosine operators implemented through quantum singular value transformation. By encoding the square root of the stationary density, the model enables sampling through computational-basis measurements and is efficiently controlled by coefficients of the block-encoded diagonal potential. We train the proposed model to match empirical distributions within the family described by FP equations, demonstrat-ing parameter recovery for multivariate probability distributions. Crucially, the model remains trainable at scale. Sample-efficient and convex optimization for in-distribution targets arises from a physics-informed inductive bias and holds regardless of the dimensionality of the problem. We provide circuit constructions and efficient block encodings of the main components of the algorithm and analyze resource requirements. Our approach frames quantum generative learning as quan-tum model discovery and provides a foundation for extending this framework to high-dimensional, strongly correlated scientific data. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Generative modeling relies on learning a probability distribution from data and designing machine learning (ML) models that draw new samples from it [1]. Diffu-sion and flow-based models are now ubiquitous for tasks ranging from image generation to scientific discovery in materials science and high-energy physics [2–5]. Quan-tum machine learning (QML) aims to build models for the same tasks [6–8]. QML typically relies on parame-terized quantum circuits (PQCs) [9, 10], encoding data into quantum states [11] and treating circuit angles as trainable weights. Generative modeling follows naturally from quantum measurement, which is inherently proba-bilistic and obeys the Born rule, x ∼ pθ (x) = |⟨ x|ψθ ⟩| 2.Quantum circuit Born machines (QCBMs) use this di-rectly [12, 13]. An extra motivation comes from the complexity of random circuit sampling [14] and from a predicted quantum advantage for specific generative tasks [15]. Other model families include hybrid adver-sarial networks [16, 17], quantum Boltzmann machines and mixed-state models [18–22]. Spectral models have gained attention for being inherently regularized in the latent space of basis functions [23], with sampling en-abled by a fixed transform to the computational ba-sis [24–27]. Multivariate and correlated distributions have been addressed with joint-distribution circuits [28– 31] and physics-based models motivated by photonic and hybrid platforms [32–36]. The performance of QML models largely depends on architectural choices and their parameterization. The classical recipe of adding depth and parameters [37] does not transfer directly to quantum circuits [38]. Sufficiently expressive, unstructured circuits exhibit barren plateaus, where gradients vanish exponentially in the number of qubits and training from a polynomial number of mea-surements fails [39–41]. The severity depends on the ar-chitecture, on the loss and its estimation [42, 43], and on the initialization [44], while training shallow circuits can suffer from multiple local minima [45]. When training on quantum hardware, gradient estimation from finite samples becomes expensive as the number of parameters grows [46–48]. These barriers have motivated shallow and problem-informed circuits [49, 50], modified train-ing approaches [48], and protocols that train classically and reserve the quantum device for sampling [27, 51–54]. The tension between trainability guarantees and classi-cal simulability [55] further suggests changing the model parameterization and untying the number of parameters from the number of qubits [56]. Quantum algorithms for differential equations offer an-other route to preparing states and probability distribu-tions. This can be done at a cost polylogarithmic in the grid size, under assumptions on operator access, condi-tioning, and state preparation [57–60]. Non-unitary dy-namics can be treated by linear combinations of Hamil-tonian simulation or by Schr¨ odingerization [61–64]. Non-linear problems have been approached through Carle-man and Kolmogorov linearization [65, 66], latent-space physics-informed learning [67, 68], and effective Hamil-tonians whose ground state encodes the solution [69– 71]. Stochastic differential equations are a natural target, since the density of their trajectories obeys a Fokker– Planck equation [72]. Quantum algorithms for stochastic dynamics and Fokker–Planck equations have been devel-oped [24, 73–81]. While reading out a full amplitude-encoded solution can consume the speedup [82], sampling is a natural target that avoids full reconstruction. At the same time, a solver for a given operator is not yet a learned generative model. In this work we construct such a model. The trainable parameters are the coefficients of the potential of an over-damped Langevin process, and the circuit is a quantum solver that prepares the stationary distribution of the associated Fokker–Planck equation. IV. TRAINABILITY 

The optimization problem of Sec. III D differs from training a generic parameterized quantum circuit. The optimizer sees K variables regardless of the coarseness of the grid set by N or the data dimensionality d. A change of θ is passed to the circuit through O(dr ) QSVT an-gles, while the remainder of the compound circuit is set by the discretization and the solver. There is no ansatz-layer count to tune in the usual sense. The only mod-eling choice is the dictionary {vj }, which encodes prior knowledge about the physical system in the same way that a dictionary does in sparse identification of dynamics [85, 86]. Barren plateau arguments for random or highly expressive PQCs [39, 40] do not apply directly because 

θ does not parameterize a unitary drawn from an ap-proximate design. On the contrary, adding qubits in our construction refines the problem grid and improves the continuum approximation, with the gradient converging to an n-independent value. The MMD 2 loss is not in general convex in θ, even for an exponential family. > VII. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

We have proposed a quantum generative model in which the trainable variables are coefficients of the poten-tial of a stochastic differential equation and the circuit is a quantum solver for the associated Fokker–Planck prob-lem. The construction avoids the usual pitfalls of scaling variational quantum machine learning models: the circuit is fixed and algorithmically structured, and the model is parameterized at the level of the differential operator it block encodes. The number of trainable parameters is set by the physics, is independent of the grid, and can remain small while the prepared state occupies an ex-ponentially large configuration space. The optimization problem is closer to model discovery than to distribu-tion fitting: the optimum is a drift field, likelihood-based training is convex in the natural parameters because the stationary state is an exponential family, and the opti-mum is characterized by K moments of the data. The approximations of the solver (discretization, finite imagi-nary time, finite quadrature order) are controllable, with the learned model converging to the exact one as they are refined. The numerical results support each of these points in one dimension and, with coupled potentials, in two and three dimensions, within classical emulation. The regime in which the architecture is most attractive is that of many coupled stochastic degrees of freedom, for which a d-dimensional grid with N points per axis is out of reach for classical grid-based methods but occupies 

d log 2 N qubits. The two- and three-dimensional exam-ples of Sec. V already use coupling terms, and the block encoding construction does not incur qubit or gate costs exponential in the problem dimension. Couplings of this kind are well established on the classical side. Quartic couplings x2 

> i

x2 

> j

and Ginzburg–Landau potentials produce multimodal, non-Gaussian correlated states in coarse-grained molecular [86] and field-theoretic models [106]. More generally, non-Gaussian pairwise Markov random fields have the same polynomial exponential-family form, and identifying their non-zero couplings is a central task in statistical learning [107]. A PQC-based quantum circuit Born machine trained with the same data would return a set of gate angles with no direct physical meaning. Here the target param-eters θ∗ make up the coefficient vector of a drift field, and the associated overdamped Langevin equation with 

V (·; ˆθ) is a stochastic model of the data that can be sim-ulated forward, perturbed, or inspected. Identifiability is inherited from the exponential-family structure: if the data-generating potential lies in the span of the dictio-nary and σ is known, the maximum-likelihood estimator 

ˆθ converges to the true coefficients θ∗ as nm → ∞ . If the dictionary is misspecified, ˆθ is the moment projec-tion of p∗ onto the model family, which is still the best stochastic model of the given form. Regarding classical simulation hardness, the Hilbert space dimension alone does not establish a quantum ad-vantage. Identifying the boundary of classical simulation requires an end-to-end comparison with state-of-the-art classical sampling methods for the same family of dis-tributions. On the quantum side, this comparison must account for the state-preparation cost of Sec. VI, includ-ing the gap dependence of t and the post-selection suc-cess probability (App. C). It needs to include the sam-ple complexity of the loss and the number of optimiza-tion steps. On the classical side, the relevant bench-mark is the best available sampler for the same fam-ily. In the small-noise, multi-well regime, mixing can be the main bottleneck, rather than grid size [91, 92]. The present work provides a model architecture for this comparison: a physics-informed generative model with a low-dimensional trainable parameter space and a struc-tured state-preparation circuit inherited from quantum differential equation solvers. The framework hence opens a route to identifying regimes in which quantum state preparation offers an advantage over classical sampling.
