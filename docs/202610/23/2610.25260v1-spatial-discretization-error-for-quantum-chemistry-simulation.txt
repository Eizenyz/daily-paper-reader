Abstract 

Nonrelativistic quantum chemistry is based on the Schr¨ odinger equation in continuous, un-bounded space. Computational approaches on classical and quantum computers often truncate the spatial domain and introduce a finite-dimensional basis, reducing the continuum problem to a finite-dimensional one. There is therefore a fundamental question: to what extent does this approximation capture the original continuum problem? In this work, we develop a general Sobolev-Galerkin framework for estimating spatial discretization errors in quantum simulation with unbounded Hamiltonians. We apply this framework to many-body Coulomb systems and establish both periodization and Galerkin error estimates for wave-function dynamics and ground-state energies. For dynamics, the finite-basis projection error of the exact wave function does not capture the full basis-discretization error: an additional dynamical projection error enters our bound at a different rate, and we introduce an inner-product argument to control this contribution. Interestingly, the spatial convergence rates for wave-function dynamics and ground-state energies differ quantitatively. For ground-state energies, we establish a sharp M −1

convergence rate at fixed cell size, where M is the number of one-particle basis functions. We also show that a uniform M −1 convergence rate is impossible for the corresponding real-time L2

wave-function error with general H2 initial data. The analysis relies on many-body Hardy-type inequalities and Sobolev norm estimates with explicit polynomial dependence on system size, together with quantitative localization bounds. Combining these estimates with state-of-the-art quantum algorithms, we obtain, to our knowledge, the first rigorous end-to-end complexity bounds for quantum simulation of molecular and electronic dynamics with all spatial errors explicitly controlled. The required number of basis functions is determined directly from the continuum problem, without regularizing the underlying interactions.

# 1 Introduction 

Quantum chemistry simulation, including the simulation of electronic and molecular systems, has long been considered one of the most important applications of quantum computing. Many-body Coulomb Hamiltonians provide a first-principles description of these systems, central to problems 2in electronic structure, chemical dynamics, and materials science [1–4]. Developing efficient quan-tum algorithms for these tasks and analyzing their computational complexity have been central themes in quantum computing. Pioneering contributions include quantum algorithms for energy estimation and chemical dynamics [5–8]. Recent years have witnessed significant progress in quan-tum algorithms and circuits for these tasks, in both first and second quantization. These seminal developments include improved algorithms for energy estimation and real-time dynamics [9–17], as well as efficient circuit implementations and initial-state preparation [18–24]. These contribu-tions have also led to significant advances in resource estimation for applications including reaction mechanisms, battery materials, and stopping-power calculations for inertial fusion [25–29]. The costs of these algorithms are usually expressed in terms of the number of basis functions (or spatial discretization grids) in a finite-dimensional Hamiltonian. To be specific, these algorithms are formulated in a chosen finite basis, whose size is treated as an independent input parameter, and their costs are analyzed for the resulting finite-dimensional Hamiltonian. For the underlying continuum problem, however, this number is not an independent input. It must be chosen according to the required spatial accuracy, which depends on the particle number, simulation time, target precision, and physical scales of the system. A rigorous complexity bound for the continuum problem requires an error estimate that makes these dependences explicit. For many-body Coulomb systems, this is particularly challenging because the interaction is both singular and long-ranged. Controlling spatial discretization errors is essential for real-space quantum simulation, where the underlying continuum Hamiltonian is an unbounded operator. Within quantum computing, pioneering efforts include rigorous spatial discretization analyses of the dynamics for bounded po-tentials using finite-difference methods [30] and Fourier spectral methods [31]. The importance of this issue is highlighted by the observation that “under some circumstances these errors can remove the exponential speedups typically afforded by quantum simulation” [30]. These prior analyses (1) assume bounded potentials and replace the Coulomb interaction by a smooth, regularized version that removes its singularity; (2) start from finite periodic domains; and (3) take regularity or mo-mentum bounds on the wave function throughout the simulation as given inputs, without deriving their dependence on the initial data and system parameters. In particular, the Fourier spectral analysis retains a parameter controlling high-order derivatives of the wave function itself. Deriving these derivative bounds with explicit dependence on the initial data and system parameters, and choosing the regularization parameter needed to control the error relative to the original Coulomb dynamics, both require separate analysis. Complementary progress includes discontinuous Galerkin representations that combine compact basis sets with structured interactions for efficient quantum simulation [32]. While prior work on regularized Coulomb interactions provides important insights, the original Coulomb Hamiltonian underlying the continuum chemistry problem poses a fundamentally different analytical problem. In addition to the high dimensionality of the many-body system, its spatial approximation must address both the Coulomb singularity and the long-range interaction. The singularity limits the regularity available for approximation, while the long range makes the error introduced by periodic images nontrivial. The Coulomb singularity can cause a loss of spatial regularity of the quantum states. In particular, Sobolev regularity of order s > 5/2 need not be preserved under Coulomb evolution. Similarly, in the ground-state problem, the hydrogenic ground state does not possess H5/2 regularity [33]. Thus, the high-order derivative bounds required by the preceding analyses cannot generally be assumed to persist for unregularized Coulomb states, and their spatial error bounds do not directly extend to the original Coulomb problem. Establishing quantum complexity bounds for the original continuum Hamiltonian therefore requires a spatial 3error analysis that controls both the finite-domain and finite-basis errors, with explicit dependence on the initial data and system parameters. A separate question concerns the spatial error in ground-state energy estimation. Even when the spatial error in real-time dynamics is controlled, an L2 wave-function error bound does not by itself determine the convergence rate of the discretized ground-state energy. These are different approximation problems, and their spatial convergence rates need not coincide. A complementary line of work analyzes quantum simulation and Trotter errors for unbounded Hamiltonians [34–36]. For many-body Coulomb dynamics, recent analyses work directly in contin-uous space without regularizing the interaction and derive Trotter error bounds valid for all initial states in the domain of the Hamiltonian, with explicit dependence on the particle number [37, 38]. Further improvements under additional assumptions on the initial state are obtained in [38]. These analyses also provide, as byproducts, many-body Coulomb-Hardy estimates and Sobolev propaga-tion bounds, which control the singular interaction and the regularity of the evolving wave function. These results are important for spatial error analysis: even when approximating a fixed function, its Sobolev regularity controls the available convergence rate, while the corresponding Sobolev norms enter the error bound and hence determine how many basis functions are sufficient to achieve a prescribed accuracy. In this work, we address the following three questions: 1. How can we estimate the spatial discretization error for many-body Coulomb Hamiltonians, including both the finite computational domain and the finite number of basis functions ?2. Can the spatial errors in wave-function dynamics and ground-state energies be analyzed within a common framework, and do they exhibit the same convergence rate? 3. How do these estimates determine the end-to-end complexity of quantum simulation when the basis size is chosen from the target continuum accuracy, rather than supplied as an independent parameter? We answer all these questions affirmatively. We develop a general Sobolev-Galerkin framework for estimating spatial discretization errors in quantum simulation with unbounded Hamiltonians. We apply this framework to many-body Coulomb systems, treating both real-time wave-function simulation and ground-state energy estimation. We first consider η electrons interacting with fixed nuclei at positions Rκ with charges Zκ > 0. In atomic units, the whole-space Hamiltonian and its evolution are

The framework accounts for both finite-domain and finite-basis errors. While the finite-domain error has received comparatively less attention in the literature, controlling it is essential for con-necting finite-domain simulations to the original whole-space problem. Crucially, the finite-basis projection error of the exact wave function does not (!) capture the full error of the Galerkin evolution. Alongside this standard approximation error, one must control a dynamical projection 4error: the discrepancy between projecting the exact evolution and evolving under the projected Hamiltonian. This contribution requires analysis specific to the Hamiltonian. For smooth bounded potentials, the two contributions can have the same convergence rate, making the distinction less apparent. We develop an inner-product argument that retains the differential inequality for the squared dynamical error, including its dependence on the error itself. This allows us to use the regularity of the dynamical error, rather than estimate the coupling only through its forcing norm, and leads to a sharper convergence bound for Coulomb dynamics. For ground-state energies, we establish a sharp M −1 basis convergence rate at fixed cell size, where M is the number of one-particle basis functions. This rate is optimal, as it matches the one-body asymptotics [39] and agrees with earlier expectations [10, Section I.A] and numerical evidence [40]. Yet the fixed-cell rate does not imply that M = O(ε−1) suffices for the original whole-space problem: the cell size must also be chosen according to the target accuracy, and its dependence enters the error bound. Moreover, the contrast with real-time dynamics is sharper still. We show that a uniform M −1 rate is impossible for the real-time L2 wave-function error with general H2 initial data. Thus, the spatial errors, even the basis convergence rates themselves, differ between ground-state energy estimation and wave-function dynamics. This distinction leads to different spatial parameter choices and, ultimately, different end-to-end quantum complexities.

## 2.3 Outline of the Estimates 

To obtain a complete end-to-end complexity estimate, we rigorously bound all sources of spatial approximation error. We divide the spatial approximation into the following two steps. First, we estimate the finite-domain truncation error incurred by replacing the whole-space problem with a problem on a finite computational domain and omitting the portion of the wave function outside this domain. For many spatial discretization methods, such as plane-wave basis, this reduction is naturally accompanied by the imposition of periodic boundary conditions. We refer to this combined procedure as finite-domain truncation and periodization, as commonly termed in numerical analysis. In the physics and chemistry literature, the corresponding construction is commonly referred to as the supercell approximation. This error is controlled by Sobolev flow estimates for the whole-space dynamics, as developed in [37], together with localization estimates for the solution. An important point here is to make the system-size dependence of the Sobolev estimates explicit. The second part consists of the Galerkin approximation, which projects the finite-domain but still infinite-dimensional continuum problem onto a finite basis set. The resulting truncation er-ror is controlled by Sobolev flow estimates for the dynamics on the finite domain, together with approximation estimates for the chosen Galerkin space. We develop these estimates in detail below.

## 4.1 Periodic Sobolev and Hardy-type Estimates 

The periodization analysis above compares the whole-domain evolution with the periodic continuum dynamics generated by Hℓ. We now turn to the Galerkin approximation of this periodic problem, which requires quantitative Sobolev control of the periodic evolution. The whole-domain Hardy and Sobolev estimates established above cannot simply be relabeled as periodic. On the torus, the Coulomb interaction is represented by the mean-zero periodic Green function Gℓ in Eq. (54), whose local Coulomb singularity is accompanied by an additional bounded contribution depending on the cell size ℓ. # 8 Conclusion 

In this work, we developed a general Sobolev-Galerkin framework for estimating spatial discretiza-tion errors in quantum simulation with unbounded Hamiltonians. Our analysis determines the computational domain and number of basis functions from the continuum problem and target accu-racy, rather than treating the basis size as an independent input. For many-body Coulomb systems arising in quantum chemistry, we established periodization and Galerkin estimates with explicit polynomial dependence on system size, without regularizing the interactions. We combine these 66 estimates with state-of-the-art quantum algorithms such as qubitization, interaction picture simu-lation, and Trotter formulas. This gives, to our knowledge, the first rigorous end-to-end complexity bounds for real-time electronic and coupled electron-nuclear dynamics with all spatial errors explic-itly controlled. For fixed nuclei, the sharper spatial error estimates derived directly for ground-state energies also yield end-to-end complexity bounds for ground-state energy estimation. We believe that the leading ground-state periodization rate, of order ℓ−3 in the periodic cell size 

ℓ, is sharp for the periodic Coulomb Hamiltonian considered here. Even exponential localization does not remove the order-ℓ−3 quadratic correction that remains locally after subtracting the scalar term, as shown in Lemma 4. By contrast, the ground-state basis-truncation rate is Λ −3 

> m

≍ ℓ3/M ,with Λ m denoting the smallest omitted physical momentum. This cutoff power cannot be improved in general for uncorrected plane-wave Ritz energies, as the one-body eigenvalue asymptotics in [39, Theorem 1.3] give a matching lower bound when the leading coefficient is nonzero. We expect the same cutoff rate to be sharp in the many-body case. The present system-size dependence, however, is not expected to be optimal. In particular, exploiting fermionic antisymmetry may lead to sharper many-body Hardy and Sobolev estimates. Our framework makes explicit how these estimates enter the spatial errors, the required number of basis functions, and the final gate complexity. Improvements in these analytic estimates can therefore be translated into corresponding improvements in the end-to-end bounds. Technically, our periodization analysis addresses an essential source of error left unquantified in the earlier periodic-domain analyses discussed above, namely the finite-domain truncation of the whole-space Coulomb problem. We provide a pathway from the infinite domain to a finite periodic cell and a mechanism for quantifying the resulting error, combining localization estimates with the local Green function expansion to control both the wave-function tail and the change in the Coulomb interaction. To address basis truncation, we propose a framework for estimating spatial discretization error by leveraging the inner product structure of the error equation. In the Coulomb setting, this formulation allows the singular potential estimate to act on the error within the retained basis rather than the omitted component. This preserves the omitted component’s full projection decay and, together with Sobolev interpolation, improves on a direct norm estimate under the same H2 regularity assumption. The ground-state energy analysis also makes transparent the cancellation mechanism arising from the variational structure, which eliminates terms linear in the omitted component. Combining this cancellation with the additional decay supplied by the eigenvalue equation and the high-frequency Coulomb estimate yields faster basis convergence. As a byproduct, we highlight the particular importance of the choice of error metric for unbounded Hamiltonians, since different metrics can lead to fundamentally different qualitative error behavior, as demonstrated in our case. Specifically, at fixed cell size, the ground-state energy admits an M −1

convergence rate, whereas a uniform rate of this order is impossible for the L2 wave-function error with general H2 initial data; see Section 4.4. Recent end-to-end frameworks have been developed for algorithmic tasks on finite-dimensional second-quantized Hamiltonians, including thermal property estimation [48]. An important direc-tion for future work is to obtain end-to-end complexity bounds for second-quantized algorithms by extending this spatial error analysis to other orbital bases, including Gaussian and Slater orbitals, discontinuous Galerkin representations [32], and gausslets [49, 50]. The underlying Sobolev-Galerkin formulation is not specific to Coulomb interactions or plane waves and provides a starting point for these extensions. Each basis requires separate estimates for the state projection error and the coupling between retained and omitted modes, together with control of any additional approxima-tion to the interaction. Such an analysis would allow different bases and quantum algorithms to 67 be compared at the same continuum accuracy, accounting for both the required basis size and the simulation cost. A further direction is to compare the error and complexity of discretizing the Hamiltonian before Trotterization with those of discretizing the factors arising from continuum Trotterization. Bridging these two approaches requires spatial error estimates for the individual factors, in addition to Trotter error bounds. Motivated by existing Trotter analyses for continuum Coulomb Hamiltonians [37, 38] and energy-specific product-formula analyses [51, 52], a natural question is whether a direct analysis of the Trotter energy error can establish faster convergence than estimates based on wave-function errors. Such an improvement could lead to sharper end-to-end complexity bounds.
