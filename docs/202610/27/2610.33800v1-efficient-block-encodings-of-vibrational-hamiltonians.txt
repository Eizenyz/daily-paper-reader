Simulation of molecular vibrations is emerging as a promising application of quantum computing in quantum chemistry. In this work, we develop new strategies for block-encoding the second-quantized n-mode representation of the vibrational Hamiltonian, whose algebraic structure closely parallels that of the second-quantized electronic Hamiltonian. We derive linear-combination-of-unitaries (LCU) representations of this Hamiltonian based on Pauli decomposition and tensor hy-percontraction (THC) and construct efficient circuits for the corresponding block encodings. We also extend the Block-Invariant Symmetry Shift (BLISS) technique to vibrational Hamiltonians, yielding a lower LCU 1-norm and, consequently, a lower overall circuit cost. For the molecules considered here, BLISS-THC reduces the Toffoli cost of quantum phase estimation by more than two orders of magnitude relative to existing block-encoding approaches. In comparison with recent Trotter-based methods, it shows up to a ninefold reduction in the T-gate count, with the relative savings tending to increase with system size. Asymptotically, and up to polylogarithmic factors, both the Pauli and THC LCUs trade additional ancillas for a quadratic improvement in scaling with the number of modes, while THC provides an additional quadratic improvement in scaling with the number of modals. These results highlight the importance of Hamiltonian factorizations and Toffoli–qubit tradeoffs for reducing the computational cost of quantum simulations of molecular vibrations. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Understanding molecular dynamics from first principles has many applications across chemistry, including molecular identification from vibrational spectra [1, 2] and prediction of reaction pathways [3, 4]. For example, the infrared spectrum not only functions as a molecular fingerprint for identifying unknown species, but it also dictates a molecule’s ability to absorb thermal radiation, which can be used to evaluate its broader atmospheric and environmental impact [5, 6]. Within the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, nuclear and electronic motion are separated, allowing the construction of a molecular vibrational Hamiltonian from the electronic potential energy surface (PES) evaluated at various nuclear geometries. Accurate construction of electronic PESs has long been a central goal of ab initio 

molecular simulations. While achieving accurate PESs is essential, the subsequent vibrational structure problem remains a formidable computational challenge for many systems of interest. Indeed, for floppy systems such as water clusters, the key bottleneck in obtaining accurate vibrational spectra often lies not in the quality of the PES but rather in solving the vibrational Schr¨ odinger equation [7, 8]. This bottleneck motivates the need for further development of efficient algorithms for vibrational structure calculations. Assuming a PES has been obtained to sufficient accuracy, various classical computational methods exist to describe the evolution of nuclei on the PES [9, 10] or to obtain the associated vibrational spectra [11–13]. However, as the number of vibrational modes and the strength of their coupling grow, these techniques become prohibitively expensive [2]. While quantum computing has been proposed and studied extensively for the electronic structure problem [14, 15], its application to the vibrational problem remains comparatively underexplored. Encoding the bosonic representation of the vibrational Hamiltonian natively on a bosonic quantum computer has been explored in Refs. [16–18]. While such an approach enables encoding the underlying Hilbert space without truncation, bosonic quantum hardware is still in the early stages of development. In this work, we focus instead on simulating vibrational systems on qubit-based digital quantum devices. Over the last few years, numerous proposals for utilizing near-term quantum computers to calculate vibrational spectra have emerged [19–24]. Most of these methods rely on the variational principle or subspace expansion tech-niques and are limited to small molecules. They inherit the well-known difficulties of near-term methods [25] and struggle as the density of states and anharmonicity increase deeper in the spectrum. More recently, sample-based quantum diagonalization methods have also been proposed for vibrational problems [26, 27]. Interest in fault-tolerant proposals has been on the rise as well, with the majority of work [2, 28–31] focusing on utilizing the Trotter ap-proximation for Hamiltonian time evolution within quantum phase estimation (QPE) [32]. Although the Trotter approximation is space-efficient, using it for energy estimation or dynamics with guaranteed error bounds [33] often yields resource estimates that are overly pessimistic by many orders of magnitude [34]. The authors of Refs. [2, 31] arXiv:2610.33800v1 [quant-ph] 24 Oct 2026 2utilize a perturbative estimate of Trotter error [35] to evaluate the resources required for vibrational time evolution. While such estimates are known to be tighter than commutator-based upper bounds, evaluating and validating them for large vibrational systems remains computationally demanding. Despite the recent appearance of highly optimized computational approaches for estimating such errors [36], a more fundamental question remains: are Trotter methods the most efficient route for vibrational simulations? Qubitization [37], on the other hand, encodes the energies of a Hamiltonian exactly, up to rotation synthesis errors that are common to any digital quantum simulation approach. It works by transforming a block-encoded Hamiltonian expressed as a linear combination of unitaries (LCU) [38] into a Szegedy walk operator [39], whose eigenphases encode the arccosine of the rescaled eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian. This enables estimating the energies by directly extracting the phase of the walk operator, achieving Heisenberg-limited scaling [40]. Moreover, given access to an efficient block-encoding oracle, quantum signal processing [41] achieves better asymptotic scaling than Trotterization for approximating time evolution. Consequently, there is an increasing need to develop efficient techniques for block-encoding vibrational Hamiltonians. A recent study in Ref. [31] highlighted how different representations of the vibrational Hamiltonian enable distinct strategies for encoding vibrational problems on quantum computers. One of these is the real-space representation that encodes a discretized real-space grid for the vibrational coordinates into the computational basis states of the qubits. Resource estimates for block-encoding such real-space vibrational Hamiltonians have recently been analyzed in Refs. [42, 43]. In this work, we focus on an alternative representation, the second-quantized n-mode representation of the vibrational Hamiltonian [44]. It exploits an n-mode expansion of the PES along with a finite number of basis functions, often called modals, to discretize each mode. Its complexity scales with the number of modes that are allowed to be explicitly coupled to each other (discussed in detail in Section II A), making it appealing for systems that allow for an accurate description at low mode-coupling levels. This makes the use of localized modes particularly beneficial, as they have been shown to significantly reduce the need to include higher-order mode couplings [45–48]. Simulating experimental IR spectra is an interesting application of quantum simulation that can be realized by performing QPE on an initial state obtained by applying the dipole operator to a reference state [7]. The near-IR regime is a particularly interesting target because of its dense manifold of excited states and the greater prevalence of combination bands, overtones, and anharmonic effects. Since the dominant computational cost in this procedure arises from QPE, reducing the underlying Hamiltonian block-encoding cost is important for lowering the overall resource requirements. Therefore, here we consider block encodings of second-quantized vibrational Hamiltonians and assess their QPE resource requirements using ground-state energy estimation as a representative benchmark. In Ref. [49], such block-encoding-based QPE costs were evaluated using LCUs derived from the factorization of Hamiltonian tensors. The approach proposed there expresses the Hamiltonian as a sum-of-products (SOP) of one-mode operators, yielding the block encoding of the full Hamiltonian as an SOP of one-mode block encodings. While this construction is space-efficient and straightforward to generalize, it relies on repeating the block-encoding primitive for every one-mode operator. Because each primitive loads a separate, relatively short data table, it cannot fully exploit quantum read-only memory (QROM) constructions that utilize additional ancillas [50–52], whose benefit grows with table size. However, Ref. [49] explored a tradeoff between qubits and T-depth by implementing block-encoding primitives for different modes in parallel, enabled by the commutativity of disjoint mode operators. While T-depth plays an important role in the total execution time of a quantum algorithm, T-gate parallelization requires multiple magic-state factories operating simultaneously on the quantum chip alongside storage and teleportation, and is therefore tied to the specific fault-tolerant architecture. Consequently, reducing the total T-gate count serves as a direct measure of total magic state cost, while still preserving the option for subsequent parallelization if permissible under specific architectural constraints. In this work, we propose more efficient block encodings for second-quantized vibrational Hamiltonians that require fewer Toffoli gates, and consequently fewer T gates, than prior approaches for QPE-based energy estimation. We first introduce a vibrational Pauli LCU that does not involve tensor factorizations, similar to the approach in Ref. [50]. By utilizing the swap networks proposed in Ref. [53], we overcome the need for repeated application of one-mode block-encoding primitives and show that the Pauli LCU achieves a quadratic reduction in the asymptotic Toffoli cost (up to polylogarithmic factors) with respect to the number of modes compared to the best method presented in Ref. [49]. Moreover, by exploiting the permutational symmetries of the mode-coupling tensors, we extend the tensor hypercontraction (THC) [54, 55] formalism to the second-quantized form of the vibrational Hamiltonian. We explicitly formulate this for two- and three-mode-coupling interactions, while noting that the underlying factorization is generalizable to an arbitrary n-mode expansion. Under the assumption that the average THC rank scales linearly with the number of modals, and up to polylogarithmic factors, THC further reduces the asymptotic Toffoli count quadratically with respect to the number of modals. Notably, we show that modal rotations (i.e., basis rotations in the modal space) can be used to optimize vibrational block-encoding resources despite their overhead in the earlier architecture of Ref. [49]. Finally, as the total algorithmic cost scales as O(λLCU ), where λLCU is the LCU 1-norm, we extend the Block-Invariant Symmetry Shift (BLISS) technique [56] to systematically minimize λLCU . By exploiting the symmetries of the target eigenstates, BLISS modifies the Hamiltonian strictly outside the relevant symmetry 3sector to minimize λLCU without altering the desired eigenvalues. To evaluate the methods proposed here, we compare the resources required for QPE with those of the method proposed in Ref. [49] for a series of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) Hamiltonians ranging from 30 to 138 vibrational modes. These systems employ a PES truncated at the two-mode-coupling level. Additionally, we test our methods on three-mode-coupling Hamiltonians for two small molecules. We show that vibrational THC combined with BLISS achieves a reduction of more than two orders of magnitude in Toffoli count for many systems compared to prior art. For the systems studied in Ref. [31], we also demonstrate the advantages of the methods developed here over Trotterized QPE. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II establishes the necessary theoretical background. Section III introduces the new vibrational Hamiltonian LCUs and details the BLISS technique. Section IV derives the asymptotic resource estimates for the vibrational LCUs, followed by the presentation of our numerical results in Section V. Finally, Section VI concludes the paper with a discussion of open challenges and future research directions in the quantum simulation of vibrational Hamiltonians.

C. Block-Invariant Symmetry Shift (BLISS) 

Block-encoding a Hermitian operator within a unitary requires rescaling its eigenvalues, and the cost of algorithms utilizing such a block-encoding oracle scales linearly with the rescaling factor. When using an LCU-based approach, this rescaling factor is dictated by the LCU 1-norm. Reference [70] proposed a method based on Hamiltonian sym-metries to reduce this 1-norm, which was later generalized to the Block-Invariant Symmetry Shift (BLISS) technique [56, 71]. Although originally proposed for electronic Hamiltonians, the concept applies generally to any block encoding where only the action of the operator on a specific symmetry sector is relevant. In the second-quantized vibrational framework, one such symmetry sector is naturally identified by noting that physically meaningful vibrational states occupy exactly one modal per mode. Accordingly, ( ˆNi − 1) |Ψ⟩ = 0 for any physically meaningful vibrational wave-function |Ψ⟩, where ˆNi = PN −1

Care must be taken when adding killers: minimizing the 1-norm can sometimes inadvertently decrease the sparsity of the Hamiltonian tensors, potentially increasing the overall quantum resource requirements. To avoid this, we restrict the killer operators considered in this work as detailed in Appendix D. Applying BLISS to the THC framework (BLISS-THC) is complicated by the nonlinear optimization required to obtain the THC factors. We observed that performing BLISS prior to THC factorization yields tensors that re-quire substantially higher ranks to achieve the same accuracy. Simultaneous optimization of the BLISS and THC parameters, an approach used for electronic Hamiltonians [68], also suffered from the same issue. Consequently, we restrict the BLISS-THC optimization to the simplest killer operator: the one-mode-coupling shift. In this work, the BLISS-THC protocol consists of first deriving the standard THC LCU, followed by optimizing the one-mode killer coefficients to minimize λTHC . Further details are provided in Appendix D. Because BLISS primarily alters the 1-norm without changing the algebraic structure of the Hamiltonian, the block-encoding circuit remains conceptually unchanged. Provided the sparsity of the Hamiltonian is not significantly reduced, the required qubit count is also preserved.

> V. RESULTS

Following the work of Ref. [49], we evaluate the LCUs developed in this study on a series of seven polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) with 30–138 vibrational modes. Specifically, these systems are linear chains of fused benzene rings known as polyacenes. They provide a systematic means to scale problem complexity by increasing the number of benzene rings. Additional details of these molecules are provided in Appendix F. We compare our approach with the Triangular LCU. The procedure for constructing the Triangular LCU and determining the associated resource estimates is detailed in Appendix A. We analyze the two-mode-coupling Hamiltonian costs for all seven systems using five modals per mode in the vibrational self-consistent field (VSCF) basis [12]. For the three-mode-coupling Hamiltonians, we restrict our analysis to PAH1 and PAH2, employing three modals per mode. All Hamiltonians are generated using MidasCpp [73]. > VI. CONCLUSION

In this work, we have developed improved block-encoding strategies for second-quantized vibrational Hamiltonians and benchmarked their resource requirements against those of Ref. [49] for a series of polycyclic aromatic hydrocar-bon Hamiltonians. Exploiting similarities between the second-quantized electronic and vibrational Hamiltonians, we have generalized some of the block-encoding techniques developed for the electronic structure problem to vibrational quantum simulation. We have derived Pauli and THC LCUs and extended the BLISS technique to reduce the LCU 1-norm. Under the unary mapping, the vibrational creation and annihilation operators map directly to Pauli raising and lowering operators, making the construction of the corresponding qubit Hamiltonian straightforward. Obtaining the THC representation, however, involves a nonlinear optimization and a basis transformation via modal rotation unitaries. We have ensured efficient THC optimization by employing a CP3-based initialization that decouples the optimization problem for each mode. The Triangular LCU, however, uses the fewest logical qubits, with the Pauli LCU requiring only a small increase when no additional ancillas are used. Up to polylogarithmic factors, allocating sufficient additional qubits allows both the Pauli and THC LCUs to achieve a quadratic improvement in the Toffoli scaling with the number of modes. Assuming that the average THC rank scales linearly with the number of modals, THC provides an additional quadratic improvement, up to polylogarithmic factors, in scaling with the number of modals. In part, these improvements arise from the ability to better exploit Toffoli–qubit tradeoffs using ancilla-assisted QROM techniques [50–52] compared to the Triangular LCU framework. We note that recently improved QROM implementations could reduce costs further [78]. For a QPE-based workflow of two-mode-coupling Hamiltonians, our results demonstrate that BLISS-THC can achieve up to a 281-fold reduction in the total Toffoli count compared to the Triangular LCU for only a modest increase in the qubit count. A similar analysis of small three-mode-coupling Hamiltonians indicates reductions by factors of 95–178, with the asymptotic scaling suggesting larger reductions as the number of modals increases. In both cases, further reductions in Toffoli cost are possible, but only at the expense of substantially increased qubit requirements. Furthermore, when benchmarked against recent Trotter-based approaches for a set of small systems with up to 9 modes, our qubitization methods achieve reductions of up to nearly ninefold in T-gate cost. Taken together with our asymptotic resource analysis, these results identify BLISS-THC as the most resource-efficient block-encoding strategy for almost all benchmarked systems in this work. Beyond quantum simulation, the vibrational THC factorization developed here may also be useful for classical methods for computing vibrational spectra. Since its original development by Mart´ ınez and coworkers [55], THC has been used to accelerate several established electronic structure methods [79, 80]. Given the parallels between second-quantized electronic and vibrational structure, it would be interesting to investigate whether analogous THC-based improvements are possible for vibrational perturbation theory [81], vibrational configuration interaction [82], and vibrational coupled-cluster theory [83, 84]. Future work in quantum simulation could include end-to-end resource estimates for vibrational double factoriza-tion. While straightforward to formulate for two-mode-coupling Hamiltonians, this approach will require nonlinear optimization for higher-order interactions. Additionally, extending techniques such as sum-of-squares and spectral amplification, which were recently proposed for electronic Hamiltonian simulation [85], presents a promising avenue for further cost reductions. Beyond optimizing our current framework, it is also valuable to establish numerically grounded comparisons of quantum resource requirements across different representations of the same vibrational problem at a fixed target accuracy. While this study focuses on a second-quantized representation, an efficient block-encoding of a real-space representation of the rovibrational Hamiltonian was recently introduced in Ref. [43]. A comparison across such representations for Trotter-based QPE was conducted in Ref. [31], demonstrating that a second-quantized approach required fewer resources than a real-space formulation for spectral calculations considered in that work. A similar comparison for block-encoding-based QPE will be important for identifying the most efficient pathway toward 17 practical vibrational quantum simulation. Furthermore, given the rapid increase in research activity to develop fault-tolerant algorithms for molecular vibrations, there is a growing need to identify challenging target molecular systems against which algorithmic costs can be meaningfully benchmarked. Such targets are likely to be large, strongly coupled systems with potential energy surfaces that do not admit compact SOP forms or n-mode expansions, making them challenging for leading tensor-network-based methods.

The Toffoli cost of implementing the PREP circuit can be obtained as follows. 

• The circuit begins by preparing a uniform superposition over the S basis states. This operation incurs a cost of 3⌈log 2 S⌉ − 3η + 2 br − 9 [54], where η represents the largest integer such that 2 η divides S, and br denotes the number of precision bits used to specify the required rotation angles for the state preparation. 

• All the indices and flags of the LCU are then loaded using QROM. The cost of QROM with clean ancillas is given by QRM Tc (S, m ) defined in Eq. (B4), and QROM with dirty ancillas is given by QRM Td (S, m ) defined in Eq. (B6). Here m is defined as 

m = 2 n(⌈log 2 M ⌉ + ⌈log 2 Rmax ⌉) + 2 ⌈log 2 n⌉ + 2 + ℵ, (C10) where Rmax = max {N, max i Ri}. In practice, the maximum THC rank is at least N , so Rmax = max i Ri.
