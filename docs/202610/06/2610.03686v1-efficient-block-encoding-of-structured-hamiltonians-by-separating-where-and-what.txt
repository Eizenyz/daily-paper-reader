(Dated: October 5, 2026) Fault-tolerant block encodings of structured Hamiltonians can reduce their non-Clifford cost in 

Select by selecting where an operator acts separately from what is applied. We construct permute– act–unpermute circuits whose CSWAP networks exploit the support geometry: a selected support is brought to a fixed target register, a shared local circuit acts there, and the permutation is undone. For supports of fixed size, the T count of Select then grows with the system size rather than with the number of terms, without requiring translational symmetry or factorised coefficients. For two-site supports, we establish and attain the minimum CSWAP count within address-controlled networks of site transpositions. Compiled for Pauli terms, these circuits use 8S N + O(log N ) T gates with 

O(log N ) permutation work qubits, where N is the number of system qubits and S is determined by the support geometry, with S = 1 for nearest-neighbour interactions and S = 3 /2 for all-to-all pairs. We also introduce a bridged CSWAP that retains and repairs a temporary AND across the target action. When the repair is Clifford, it halves the T count of a matched forward–inverse CSWAP pair at the cost of one retained work qubit. For a Heisenberg ring, the complete block-encoding query reduces the T count by a factor approaching 3 as the system size grows. For a five-orbital Anderson impurity model, which combines nearest-neighbour and all-to-all support geometries, the reduction is about 1 .7 at large bath sizes. Both comparisons use the lowest-cost compiled baselines considered, at unchanged block-encoding normalisation and comparable peak ancilla counts. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Simulating quantum many-body systems has motivated quantum computation since Refs. [1, 2], and it remains among the most promising applications of fault-tolerant machines. In the quantum singular value transformation of Ref. [3], which generalises the quantum signal pro-cessing of Ref. [4], state preparation, time evolution and spectral functions, Refs. [5–9], are all obtained as poly-nomials of a Hamiltonian block-encoded as in Ref. [10], so the non-Clifford cost of the block encoding is paid at every query. In the linear-combination-of-unitaries (LCU) approach of Refs. [11–15], Prepare loads the normalised term weights into a label register and Select applies the unitary labelled there. Generic constructions, such as the unary iteration of Ref. [16], treat the terms as a flat list and pay a T count proportional to their number. Many Hamiltonians of interest have more structure than a list of their terms. A small repertoire of local operators, such as fields, hopping or exchange terms, pla-quette operators and density–density interactions, recurs over many supports arranged by a geometry. Examples are the nearest-neighbour or finite-range bonds of the lat-tice models of Refs. [17, 18], the plaquettes of the lattice gauge theory of Ref. [19], and the all-to-all power-law cou-plings of the Rydberg-atom arrays reviewed in Ref. [20]. Many models combine several such families, as an impu-rity model attaches bath chains to an all-to-all core. Moving selected degrees of freedom to common work po-sitions has precedents in real-space simulation, Ref. [21], fermionic Select , Ref. [22], and first-quantised simula-tion, Refs. [23, 24]. Related chemistry block encodings exchange the two spin sectors of the system register under the control of a single prepared bit, Refs. [25, 26], while Ref. [19] expresses a lattice-gauge block encoding in this form. What differs is the cost that the permutation is built to minimise. Refs. [22–24] select the sites of a term independently, with one swap network per site or register. Ref. [22] builds this network ancilla-free, at logarithmic depth, and Ref. [23] uses it to run the arithmetic once, in ancilla registers. Here, instead, the permutation is built from as few CSWAPs as the structure of the supports allows. This split makes the costs of where and what add instead of multiplying. When every set of nT sites may be a support, the number of terms grows as N nT times the number of operator classes, and flat unary iteration pays about 4 T for each term. For the Pauli LCUs of this paper, the operator is an XZ mask on T alone, applied at a cost that does not grow with N . The register-wide mask of Ref. [27] instead spans all N qubits and needs 2N mask qubits. The permutation is thus the only stage that touches all N system qubits. It depends on the address alone, so every operator class reuses it, and its CSWAP count stays linear in N for any fixed nT . Select 

therefore costs O(N ) T gates however many terms there are. Their number enters the query only through the coefficients that Prepare loads. For the Heisenberg ring, six Pauli terms per site make a flat walk cost 24 N T gates at leading order, against 8 N for the permutation. The permutation therefore carries the leading cost, and the geometry of the supports sets it. We build it from the CSWAP network of the SelectSwap lookup of Ref. [28]. That lookup swaps classical data words into an output register. Our networks move system qubits instead, so the support geometry sets their structure. Select then costs 8S N + O(log N ) T gates, where the CSWAP density S depends on the geometry alone. It is 1 for finite-range bonds and plaquettes in any dimension, and 3 /2 for all-to-all pairs. For pair supports, we prove that this CSWAP count is the minimum over address-controlled networks of site transpositions. Selecting the two sites jointly, instead of with one swap network per site, pays even without locality. The matched forward and inverse permutations allow a second saving, in time rather than across terms. Each CSWAP forms a temporary AND of its control and the parity of its two qubits, at 4 T , as reconstructible from Refs. [16, 29, 30]. The target action may change the parity, so the value is normally erased before the action and recomputed after it. When the prepared labels fix this change, the value can instead be kept live and repaired. We call the result a bridged CSWAP. It halves the cost of a matched pair when the repair is Clifford, at the price of one work bit kept live across the action. Permute–act–unpermute, thus, is based on the com-pilation of the permutation from the structure of the Hamiltonian, and a Prepare that writes the address in the form the CSWAP network consumes. We apply this approach to two Hamiltonians to test the two ends of the structure geometry: the Heisenberg ring, with nearest-neighbour bonds, and the Anderson impurity model (AIM), which attaches bath chains to an all-to-all impurity core. As the ring grows, and as the bath grows at a fixed number of impurity orbitals, their complete queries asymptotically need one third and three fifths of the T gates of the cheapest matched compiled comparator, respectively, at comparable peak workspace. Sec. II builds Select , the bridged CSWAP and the resource law, and Sec. III applies them to the two models. App. A constructs the permutation networks and proves their CSWAP bound, and App. B gives the target actions and the coefficient sampler. Apps. C and D assemble and count the complete queries.  

> II. PERMUTE–ACT–UNPERMUTE SELECT

This section constructs a Select for structured Hamil-tonians whose T count does not grow with the number of terms. Three ideas achieve this. First, each term is split into where it acts and what it applies. Second, the geometry of the supports fixes the cost of where . Third, a temporary value of the forward permutation is kept and repaired for the inverse permutation, instead of being recomputed. The subsections follow the questions that this construc-tion raises. Sec. II A explains why a flat list of terms is costly. It then splits each term into a permutation and a target action. Counting the resulting cost needs a unit. Sec. II B fixes it as the temporary-AND opening and prices a CSWAP in these units. Sec. II C counts the CSWAPs of the permutation. It shows that the geometry of the supports fixes their number. The CSWAPs and the target action also need controls. Sec. II D specifies what Prepare writes for them and what the remaining control logic costs. Sec. II E combines these pieces into the cost with every CSWAP pair closed. This baseline pays two openings per pair. Sec. II F removes one of them by repairing the work bit of each CSWAP instead of recomputing it. The price is one work bit kept live per pair treated this way.

> C. The support geometry fixes the permutation

The permutation is the only stage that touches every system qubit. Its number of CSWAPs therefore sets the leading cost of Select .The basic building block is a swap tree , the CSWAP network of Ref. [28]. A swap tree acts on M sites, held at its leaves , which are numbered by the address that selects them. With M − 1 CSWAPs, it brings the site at the selected leaf to leaf zero, the root of the tree, so the root is one of the M sites and not an extra qubit. The CSWAPs are arranged in ⌈log 2 M ⌉ layers, one per address bit. Each layer halves the range in which the selected site can sit. For M a power of two, the layers thus hold 

M/ 2 + M/ 4 + · · · + 1 = M − 1 CSWAPs. For other M ,the swaps that would reach outside the range are omitted, and the count stays M −1. Fewer CSWAPs cannot suffice. Run backwards from the root, each CSWAP adds at most one site to those that can reach it. Every CSWAP is controlled by a single address bit, as sketched in Fig. 2(d) and drawn in full in Fig. 12. If every support is a single site, T is one qubit and Πj is a single swap tree over all 4

N sites, with that qubit as its root. A two-site support needs one site on each qubit of T , so the permutation must combine several trees such that all sites of the selected support arrive together. The support geometry sets how many CSWAPs the permutation needs, and the choice of trees can lower that number. For nearest-neighbour bonds, selecting the two ends of a bond independently uses one tree per end. A first tree over all N sites brings site j to q0. A second tree over all sites brings site j + 1 to q1. Every site then sits in both trees, and Πj holds about 2 N CSWAPs. The bond structure makes half of this work unnecessary. Every bond joins an even site to an odd site. A tree over the even sites alone can therefore bring the even end of the bond to q0. A tree over the odd sites alone brings the odd end to q1. Each site now belongs to a single tree, and the two trees hold N − 2 CSWAPs, about half as many as before. The only price is that the two ends of the bond can arrive swapped. Which end lands on q0 now depends on the parity of j. More generally, a network brings the sites of the selected support to T , but which site lands on which qubit of T may depend on the address. The CSWAP counts of this subsection leave that assignment free. An exchange on T controlled by address bits can fix it. Alternatively, the target action can read the low address bits that record where each site landed and act on the right qubits directly, as the Anderson impurity model does in Sec. III B. Either way the extra cost acts on T alone and does not grow with N .The same idea applies whenever the sites split into groups such that no support holds two sites of the same group. > D. What Prepare supplies to Select

The permutation and the target action read their con-trols from registers that Prepare writes. The form of these registers decides how much control logic Select 

must still compute. We fix that form by three choices. First, Prepare writes the address in the form that the network reads. A swap tree reads the leaf of the selected site, its position within its group, not its index in the system. Converting one into the other may require a few products of address bits. A network that ranges over many tree roots may need further products, such as the retained products of all-to-all pairs, whose number Sec. A 5 establishes at every N up to one opening. We 5call all these products nonlinear controls and write N pred 

for their number. Each costs one opening. A control generator G computes them first, and G† erases them at the end. Second, the target action reads the exponents of Eq. (4) from label bits. Prepare can write the label in two forms. A compressed label is as short as the binary logarithm of the number of operator classes. An explicit mask holds all 2nT exponents, which adds 8 nT T to coefficient sampling. Sec. B 1 traces this charge to coherent alias sampling, which spends one opening on each output bit. When each exponent is a label bit or a parity of label bits, it controls one CNOT or one CZ on a target qubit, and P is Clifford. An explicit mask always gives this case. A compressed label may also need a few products of label bits, at a cost independent of N . Either way, the cost of what does not grow with N .Third, one block encoding can combine several struc-tured families, each with its own permutation, target register and target action. The prepared label flags ex-actly one family in each branch and sets all exponents read by P to zero for the others. Each inactive family then acts as the identity: its target action is trivial, and its permutation and inverse cancel for any address. The fam-ily circuits can therefore run sequentially without outer controls, although their costs add because every branch executes all permutations. This latter construction resem-bles the one of Ref. [32]. The Anderson impurity model of Sec. III B combines its bath chains and all-to-all impurity core in this way.

> F. The bridged CSWAP retains its temporary AND

A closed pair spends half of its cost on recomputation. The forward CSWAP computes the work bit ω = c (q0⊕q1)and closes it, and the inverse CSWAP computes the same product again. Yet an exchange leaves the pair parity 

q0 ⊕ q1 unchanged, so after the forward CSWAP the work bit is still valid. Between the two occurrences, only the target action can change that parity. Suppose that it flips the parity by a bit δ. The inverse CSWAP then requires 

ω′ = c (q0 ⊕ q1 ⊕ δ) = ω ⊕ c δ. (8) The forward work bit can therefore stay live across the target action, and the repair Xcδ ω updates it before the inverse CSWAP, in place of the reopening. When the repair is a Clifford gate, the pair costs one opening instead of two. We call this implementation a bridged CSWAP, Fig. 3(b), drawn compactly as in Fig. 8(a). The bridge requires only that δ be fixed by the prepared registers, not by the system state. Every Pauli target satisfies this condition, because only the X exponents of Eq. (4) flip data bits, and they depend on the label alone. For a CSWAP next to the target action, δ is the parity of the X exponents that land on its two qubits. A Z factor flips no bit, so a diagonal target needs no repair at all. An operator that superposes the two parity sectors has no fixed δ, and its CSWAPs keep the closed schedule. > III. TWO HAMILTONIAN FAMILIES INSTANTIATE THE FRAMEWORK

The construction of Sec. II is instantiated for two mod-els with complementary support geometries. In each case, we express the Hamiltonian as a structured family, identify its geometry, specify the information supplied by Prepare , determine which CSWAPs can be bridged, and count the resulting resources. The Heisenberg ring with even N ≥ 4 isolates the separation of operator class from support: its bonds have 

χ = 2, so two swap trees suffice, and no bridging is required. The Anderson impurity model combines bath chains with χ = 2 and an all-to-all impurity core in a single block encoding. Its one-body sector has both within-chain and across-chain structure, allowing repairs to be shared. It also exhibits the two useful bridging regimes of Sec. II F: a shared repair and a diagonal action requiring no repair.

We keep every CSWAP pair of the ring trees closed. Each tree swap exchanges a single qubit, so an X or 

Y action can change its retained product and require a repair predicate. A tree CSWAP sees that change only when it lies on the path of the selected site, so its predicate is a different product of address bits for nearly every tree CSWAP. Bridging the trees would therefore trade reopenings for repairs almost one for one, the same argument that keeps the AIM trees closed in Sec. III B. The resource counts below use this closed schedule, and the saving follows from the where/what split alone. We can now compare the complete query with published constructions. It costs T = 8 N + 4 μ + O(log N ). That is nearly three times fewer T gates than the best comparator, at the same peak width to within a few qubits as shown in Fig. 5. The ring comparators are evaluated as one uncontrolled call at the same normalisation, except that the register-mask row retains its published open-chain Hamiltonian. Sec. C 2 describes how each construction is adapted to Eq. (10) and how exact T gates and rotations are counted. The best comparators are the flat Pauli walk of Ref. [16] and the product-wise walk of Ref. [52], both at 24 N + 4 μ. They use our sampler Aμ, so they differ from our query only in Select .The leading factor of three is explained by the per-term cost of a flat walk. A walk over the Pauli list pays 4 T

per term, and the ring has six terms per site. The two swap trees pay 8 T per site, whatever acts there. Our permutation pays once per position, independently of the number of classes. The other two comparators are the tree Select of Ref. [48] and the register mask of Ref. [27]. Both move the non-Clifford cost into state preparation, where they pay Θ( N ) arbitrary rotations. Once synthesised, their totals lie one to two orders of magnitude higher. The register mask is evaluated on the open chain for which it was published. It also needs 2N mask qubits, the only width in Fig. 5(b) that grows linearly.  

> B. Anderson impurity model

The Anderson impurity model (AIM) describes inter-acting orbitals coupled to a non-interacting bath, Ref. [53]. It is the auxiliary problem of dynamical mean-field theory, which maps a correlated lattice onto an impurity with a self-consistent bath, Refs. [54, 55]. For real materials, the lattice model is itself derived from first principles, with localised orbitals and screened interactions, Refs. [56, 57]. Accurate bath representations require many sites, beyond the reach of exact diagonalisation. The AIM has there-fore been proposed as the quantum step of a classical embedding loop, Refs. [58, 59]. Tensor-network solvers can also treat it efficiently, Ref. [60], but rapid entangle-ment growth during real time evolution, particularly in multi-orbital systems, makes these problems promising candidates for quantum computing, Refs. [8, 61, 62]. The AIM combines the chain and all-to-all geometries of Tab. I in one Hamiltonian. Its one-body terms live on long nearest-neighbour chains. Its interactions, by contrast, couple every pair of impurity orbitals. Tab. II sets the AIM beside the ring, step by step. Its one-body family is the ring on every chain at once, plus one stage that picks the chain. Its interaction family is an all-to-all network on the impurity orbitals. > IV. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have shown that the repeated struc-ture of a Hamiltonian can be exploited to reduce the 

T count of its block encoding. The key is to separate the part of Select that decides where an operator acts from the part that decides what operator is applied. A flat walk runs through every combination of support and operator class, so its cost scales with their product. With the separation, the two costs add instead. For the Heisen-berg ring, this reduces the leading cost of Select from 24 N to 8 N T gates. For all-to-all supports of any fixed size nT , it turns the Θ( N nT ) cost of a flat walk into an 

O(N ) one. Against constructions that are already linear, the gain is a constant factor: 8 N against 10 N for the translation-invariant Hubbard construction of Ref. We also proved that the CSWAP count is minimal within this class of networks, Sec. A 2 b. For bounded connectiv-ity, part of the saving comes from how the address and operator label are represented, suggesting that the same principle may benefit other selection mechanisms. The matched forward and inverse permutations provide a second saving. Instead of erasing a temporary AND and recomputing it for the inverse permutation, a bridged CSWAP retains the value across the target action and repairs it using the prepared labels. When the repair is Clifford, this halves the T cost of a matched pair at the price of one additional ancilla. More generally, the same idea applies whenever the change of a temporary value across an operation can be inferred from information already available to the circuit. In the examples studied here, however, bridging affects only subleading terms. The dominant saving comes from separating where from 

what .We also adapted Prepare to the information that Se-lect consumes and compared complete block-encoding queries. For the Heisenberg ring, the resulting query uses about three times fewer T gates than the best comparator. For the Anderson impurity model, which combines long bath chains with an all-to-all interacting core, it uses about 1 .7 times fewer T gates than the cheapest matched comparator. These reductions are obtained at comparable peak workspace and at the same block-encoding normal-isation, so an algorithm built on these block encodings makes the same number of queries, each at the reduced cost. Several questions remain open. For supports of three or more sites, the minimum CSWAP count is known only for χ = nT , Sec. A 2 b. The nonlinear control cost remains O(log N ) for supports of fixed size, Sec. A 3 c, but whether this can always be achieved simultaneously with a minimum-size permutation network is also open. Since the advantage of separating where from what grows with the number of operator classes sharing the same supports, bond-dependent spin models and multi-orbital lattice models are natural next targets. More broadly, these results suggest that block encodings should be designed around the geometry and repeated local structure of a Hamiltonian, rather than around a flat enumeration of its Pauli terms.

Appendix A: Permutations 

This appendix derives the counts that enter Eq. (9): the CSWAPs, the nonlinear controls N pred and the re-pair openings N rep . We first give the gate identities and prove the lower bound on the number of CSWAPs, then construct the address controls and the nearest-neighbour and all-to-all networks. The final subsection derives the repairs needed to bridge gates inside these networks. 

1. Gate cost and work-bit reuse 

A temporary AND closes for free when the stored bit still equals the product of its current controls. In the circuits, a stored product begins one column after its opening. Whether the work bit of a CSWAP can be reused for its inverse depends on how the action between them changes its inputs. 

a. Keeping the work bit across the action 

For an isolated matched pair, suppose the target action changes the pair parity by δ. The inverse gate needs 

ω ⊕ cδ , so applying Xcδ ω repairs the stored bit and allows a free close, Eq. (8). This requires δ to depend only on the prepared address and label. Pauli actions satisfy this condition: their X exponents determine the bit flips, while their Z factors leave the parity unchanged. An action that superposes parity sectors has no such fixed δ

and uses the closed schedule. A diagonal action needs no repair. An affine repair predicate cδ is applied by CNOTs, so the pair again pays only one opening. A product predicate needs an opening of its own, so an isolated pair then costs two openings, as a closed one does; bridging saves only when several work bits share that product. A bridge that saves an opening saves 4 T and retains one work bit, as counted in Eq. (9). Sec. A 6 extends this rule to a network.

> 2. Swap trees and the CSWAP count

A swap tree selects one site. Combining trees for dif-ferent site groups gives the support-dependent count of Eq. (6). We construct the tree before deriving the bound that it attains for pairs.

A swap tree acts on M leaves, numbered by the address

> 3. Address controls

The transposition bound excludes the cost of its con-trols. A swap tree reads single address bits and needs no further products. Address conversion and selection among tree roots can require nonlinear controls, counted by N pred in Eq. (7). The control generator computes and retains the products that the chosen permutation network needs. A toggle is needed only when consecutive trees share a field but read different addresses.  

> a. Control generators

The control generator G computes products of prepared bits before the first permutation, and G† erases them after the inverse permutation. They close by measurement once their inputs have been restored. Products include toggle carries or borrows and network controls, counted by N pred ,and repair predicates, counted separately by N rep . This separates control generation from the order of the system permutations: an all-to-all network reads the controls directly, whereas trees that share an address field use 

Tog between stages to change the field and later restore it. A product needed in only one stage may instead be computed around that stage in each direction. This adds one opening but can reduce peak workspace, as in Sec. A 5. The trees read one position field per group, so Prepare 

supplies an address of up to χ⌈log 2 N ⌉ bits. That address is charged to Prepare , while the CSWAP count sets the leading term of Select . A network on a bounded number of tree roots contributes a bounded number of controls. A logarithmic count arises in two places. The first is where the prepared address must be converted into the fields that the trees read, as for the toggle of Sec. A 3 b. The second is where the network ranges over all sites, as for the retained products of all-to-all pairs, Sec. A 5.

> 5. All-to-all pairs

When any two sites may form a support, every group holds a single site, so χ = N and the network on the roots is the whole permutation. The complementary-pair network splits the sites into the singleton {0} and the complementary pairs {1, 2}, {3, 4}, . . . and selects the pair ( j, j ′), with j < j ′. One orientation CSWAP per complementary pair decides which of its two sites the swap tree of q1 sees and which the swap tree of q0 sees. One swap tree then brings the lower site j to q0, and the other brings the upper site j′ to q1, Fig. 15. The network spends ⌈ 32 (N − 2) ⌉ CSWAPs, five at N = 5, which meets Eq. (6) at χ = N . Its controls are XOR parities of the address bits of ( j, j ′) as long as N pre (N ) = 0, as at N = 5. Larger networks also read retained products of address bits, written by the control generator of Sec. A 3 a and counted below. The complementary-pair network reaches the CSWAP minimum at every N . Its peak helper width is Npre (N ) = max {0, ⌈log 2(N − 2) ⌉ − 2} = O(log N ) (A5) retained products of address bits, at every N . The same Npre also counts its temporary-AND openings for odd N ,for N = 2 b, 2b + 1 , 2b + 2 and N = 12 · 2r , and, by explicit verified circuits, for every remaining size up to N = 40 except N = 28. Every other even size needs at most one opening more. There each even site pairs with the odd site above it, and the N pre prefix-equality products of the pair index, the address without its lowest bit, orient these pairs as for odd N . Leaving sites 0 and N − 1 single orphans sites 1 and N − 2, which form one further pair, oriented by the product of the lowest address bits of the two endpoints: its CSWAP is enabled by that product XORed with the complement of the lowest bit of the upper endpoint. In the default schedule, the network keeps all N pre + 1 products live across both directions, at N pre + 1 openings and width N pre + 1. In the second schedule, it opens the product of the lowest bits only around the CSWAP of the pair {1, N − 2} in each direction, placed farthest from the target action so that the prefix-equality products are not live at the same time. This gives N pre + 2 openings at width N pre . At N = 28, where N pre = 3, the network holds 39 CSWAPs; the default schedule spends four open-ings at width four, and the second five openings at width three. Eq. (A5) is therefore established as a width at every N , and as an opening count at every N up to one extra opening. 

a. Fermionic signs in the all-to-all network. Summed over the all-to-all network, the strings of Eq. (A2) hold

> 6. Repairs inside a network

For a bridged gate λ inside a network, the target action is carried back through the intervening CSWAPs. Its parity change δλ(j, b ) is the XOR of the X-mask bits that reach the two positions exchanged by λ. The Z

mask never enters. The repair is therefore cλδλ, where cλ

enables the gate. Affine predicates require only CNOTs; nonlinear predicates are built from products and shared wherever possible. Their openings contribute N rep in Eq. (9). A diagonal action needs no repairs. In a swap tree, a bridged CSWAP sees a parity change only if it carries the block on which the action flips bits. For a swap tree with a single marked block, the repair factor of each bridged CSWAP is

is its layer. The bracket of a CSWAP in layer t equals 23 the bracket of the CSWAP of layer t + 1 that carries its block, or that bracket XOR its indicator, according to bit 

t + 1 of ae. Built from the top layer down, each indicator is therefore one product of at with a parity, possibly complemented, of indicators already held. It costs one opening, and the parities cost only CNOTs. In each layer the parity of all indicators is at itself, so the last indicator of a layer costs only CNOTs. The indicators of a tree thus cost one opening per CSWAP, less one per layer, and they need no lookup and no quantum flip-pattern register. When the action is itself flagged, as by the hopping flag 

fT of Sec. III B, every indicator carries the flag, and the last indicator of a layer is free only where the product of the flag with at is already held. Corrections on distinct held work bits commute with the other inverse CSWAPs, so all repairs may precede the inverse permutation. Sharing determines the saving. If a block CSWAP has 

L lanes with the same parity change, one repair predicate serves all of them: the block pays L openings plus the repair instead of 2 L. A gate that moves a single site of a pair usually needs its own path-dependent repair. Such a tree saves only those openings whose predicates follow as parities of existing products, while retaining one work bit per bridge. Both case studies therefore keep their nearest-neighbour trees closed, Secs. C 1 and D 3. 

> Appendix B: Target actions and the preparation interface

Sec. II D fixes what Prepare writes for the target action. We describe the label, the orientation and phase corrections, and the sampler that supplies them. For fixed target size and operator repertoire, the local action costs a number of T gates independent of N ; coefficient loading and any phase walks have separate costs, which can grow with N . 

> 1. Encoding and orienting the target action

An explicit XZ mask, Eq. (4), applies one CZ from 

ζq followed by one CNOT from ξq to each target qubit 

q. The action thus uses 2 nT Clifford gates and no work qubit. The X mask also determines the bridge repairs, Sec. A 6. The coefficient sign and the phase required by the Y factors are applied on the label register, as described below. The mask adds no T gate and no ancilla to Select ,as assumed in Eqs. (7) and (9), but must be written by Prepare . Extra lookup columns are Clifford; the coherent alias sampler exchanges each output bit with its alternate at one opening per bit. The 2 nT mask bits therefore add 8 nT T , independently of N and the number of supports. A compressed label uses only enough bits to distinguish the operator classes, but may require decoding in the target action. Its cost T act depends on the repertoire. If every exponent is a bit or a parity of label bits, the decoding is Clifford and T act = 0. A pair network can deliver its two sites in address-dependent order. One closed CSWAP pair on T restores their order. Alternatively, the action can use the ordering bit to apply each factor where its site arrived. This requires products of the ordering bit with the relevant label bits. It pays when the slot-dependent factor is fixed, so that one product suffices, and only ties when that factor ranges over several axes. The ring keeps the exchange because its field terms use all three axes; the AIM absorbs it because its onsite factor is always Z, Secs. C 1 and D 3.  

> 2. Signs, frames and walks

Coefficient signs and ordered-XZ phases depend only on the label. A loaded sign bit requires a Z gate. Asign computed from the label requires only Z and CZ gates when its Boolean function is at most quadratic on the populated codes; S gates supply the powers of 

i. Such a correction is Clifford, needs no ancilla, and commutes with Select , which is diagonal in the label basis. Unpopulated codes can be used to simplify the phase function. When a term carries several factors, a parity frame , a fixed CNOT layer E applied before the first permutation and after the last, can gather its X factors on fewer qubits. Only those qubits must reach a fixed target. Diagonal factors can instead be applied in place by a walk using the permutation controls. Each walk step costs one opening and closes for free because the intervening gates are diagonal. A CSWAP that places such a factor is bridged without repair when only phases act between its two occurrences, Sec. A 1 a. The AIM interaction circuit 

I uses this combination, Sec. D 4.  

> 3. The coefficient sampler

Prepare must give each term an amplitude set by its coefficient. One amplitude per term would add a lookup linear in the number of terms, which grows as the number of supports times the number of operator classes. Terms that share a magnitude, however, need it loaded only once, so the lookup grows only with the number of distinct coefficient rows, constant for the ring and linear in the bath for the AIM, and preparation alias-samples the distinct coefficient rows and uniform superpositions then pick one of the equal-weight terms of the sampled row. 5. Resources 

The leading cost comes from the one-body trees and coefficient loading. Bridging reduces the chain-front and interaction costs while retaining work bits. Both target actions and all four sign factors are Clifford. The row-address and rank superpositions supply the rotations; we count them separately from exact T gates. 

a. Permutations. The system permutations and their repairs obey Tperm ≤ 16 nimp (nbath − 1) + 12(2 nimp − 1) + 12  32 (nimp − 2)  + 4N pre (nimp ). (D13) The first term counts the closed tree CSWAPs of Eq. (D8). The second counts the bridged chain-front blocks with their shared repairs. The last two count the interaction family of Eq. (D12). The tree CSWAPs read the address bits directly. Address arithmetic costs O(log( nbath + 1)) further openings. By Eq. (A6), no repair factor needs a lookup. Preparation adds the T ref of Eq. (D7). Its only superlinear part is the rank decoder, a lookup on the O(n2imp ) pair ranks. 

b. Coefficient sampler. The remaining cost is the standard alias sampler of Ref. [16] on the R rows of Eq. (D3). It consists of a uniform superposition over exactly R row addresses, an uncontrolled unary-iteration lookup, a μ-bit comparator and a bridged exchange of the nx bits of row fields that Aμ loads, with nx = nk +

nℓ + 7. The remaining 2 nℓ + 3 fields of F are written by 

F afterwards, and they are zero during the exchange. The alternate row fields are held at the same width. Write 

R = 2 sl with l odd. The uniform state then costs N unif open =3⌈log 2 l⌉ − 1 openings and two arbitrary rotations per direction, and nothing when l = 1. The lookup costs R−2openings, one fewer than its controlled form, because the top address bit selects the two halves directly. The erasure of the lookup, the measured uncomputation of Ref. [64], first measures the loaded word, then holds only the nonlinear monomials of the ⌊nR/2⌋ low address bits and walks the ⌈nR/2⌉ high bits by unary iteration on 

⌈nR/2⌉− 1 qubits. It costs ⌈nR/2⌉− 1 more openings than holding the monomials of both halves at once, but about halves the workspace of the erasure, which then stays below the chain-front peak at every plotted size. > 6. Comparison

We compare one uncontrolled complete query, compris-ing Select and both directions of Prepare , at the same model size, coefficient precision μ and normalisation. All counts include the rotation-synthesis estimate of Eq. (C10) at εsynth = 10 −3. Workspace excludes the system register. Fig. 10 compares three constructions with ours. 

a. Comparator constructions. The flat comparator applies unary iteration from Ref. [16] to the full list of 

L = 3 N + 4 n2imp − 7nimp + 1 Pauli strings. The structured 

comparator uses the coefficient factoring and indexed se-lection of that reference, with the same R rows as Eq. (D3). The coefficients do not depend on spin, hopping axis or orbital pair, so uniform refinements supply these choices. A uniform spin bit controls CSWAPs that substitute the spin-down copies of the selection fields. A uniform coin controls a CSWAP of the two hopping indices, and its 

Select applies Y Y when they arrive in decreasing order and XX otherwise. The orbital pair is a uniform a with 

b = a + e + 1 mod nimp , for an offset e uniform over the other nimp − 1 orbitals. Its one-body selection uses two selected-Majorana passes and an indexed Z. Hund ex-change and pair hopping lie outside the four term types that this selection admits. Each factors into one two-qubit Pauli per orbital, selected by additional impurity passes. In total, the comparator uses 3 N − 5 one-body openings and six passes of nimp − 1 openings. The one-body block also exchanges its two nN -bit indices under one control bit and back, with nN = ⌈log 2 N ⌉; these 2nN CSWAPs are compiled at 7 T each, which gives the exact term 14 nN . The comparator Select thus costs 4(3 N + 6 nimp − 11) + 14 nN raw T . No pass visits all orbital pairs. The sparse comparator follows Refs. [25, 64], tabling the d = N +nimp (nimp −1) nonzero integrals of the sparse (pq |rs ) representation. Its Select applies the Majorana pair twice, costing about 16 N rather than the leading 12 N of the structured comparator. Measured lookup erasure offsets this extra selection cost. The structured comparator instead reverses its lookup at full cost, as in Ref. [16]. Each comparator is built only from the methods of the references it follows, without the constructions of this paper. 

b. Compiled checks. All four constructions are com-piled as bloqs in Ref. [65]. At (5 , 100), the compiled one-body block of the structured comparator costs 12 240 raw T , which is 4(3 N − 5) + 14 nN exactly. Our prepara-tion and the structured comparator alias-sample the R

coefficient rows of Eq. (D3), the onsite and hopping coeffi-cients of H1b , the few parameters of Hint and the constant shift η. The sparse comparator alias-samples its d nonzero integrals, and the flat comparator one amplitude per Pauli string. The flat lookup cost depends on which entries of its alias table repeat, and hence on the coefficient values, so it is compiled for generic coefficients at every plot-ted size rather than bounded. The structured lookup loads row fields, alternates and the keep word together. It also loads spin-down copies of the selection fields to implement the snake ordering, adding workspace. The structured Select was checked against every Pauli label by tensor contraction at ( nimp , n bath ) = (2 , 1); prepara-tion was checked on every basis input at nbath = 1 and 

nimp = 2 , 3, 5, and reproduces the Hamiltonian row by row. Our compiled counts agree with Sec. D 5 at every checked size. 

c. Cost and workspace. As the bath grows at fixed orbital count, our complete query asymptotically needs three fifths of the T gates of the cheapest comparator, 40% fewer. This is the ratio of the leading terms in Fig. 10(c), 24 nimp nbath here against 40 nimp nbath for the sparse and structured comparators. All four costs are linear in bath size at fixed orbital count, so the reduction is a constant factor. At five orbitals, the cheapest comparator is sparse at bath size 100 and structured at 1000, with the two costs agreeing to within two per cent. The saving is larger at small baths, where subleading terms matter, and our construction costs less at every plotted size. At five orbitals and bath sizes 100 and 1000, our query needs about three eighths of the T gates of the flat comparator. Rotation counts remain bounded as the model grows: six here, four for flat and sparse, and eight for structured. When a row count is a power of two, its row-address superposition is Clifford. Our count then drops to two and the structured count to four. This explains the dips of panel (a) at three orbitals and bath sizes 4 and 20, where our query needs about two fifths and just under half of the T gates of the cheapest comparator. Panel (c) omits these bounded synthesis costs. Panel (b) shows the workspace tradeoff. At every plot-ted size, our peak is set by the one-body chain-front permutation, with its bridged lanes and repair word live. At five orbitals and bath sizes 100 and 1000, our peak lies within seven per cent of the sparse comparator’s, an eighth to a fifth below the structured comparator’s, and about twice the flat comparator’s. The reduction in T

count does not imply a uniform reduction in workspace.
