(Dated: October 6, 2026) In fault-tolerant block encodings of structured Hamiltonians, the support geometry can set how the non-Clifford cost of Select scales with the system size, however many operators act on each support. We construct permute–act–unpermute circuits: a CSWAP network fixed by this geometry brings a selected support to a fixed target register, a shared local circuit acts there, and the permutation is undone. The operators add only an XZ mask on this register, at a cost independent of the system size, and one query can mix several support geometries. For two-site supports, we establish and attain the minimum CSWAP count within address-controlled networks of site transpositions. Compiled for Pauli terms, these circuits use 8S N + o(N ) T gates with O(log N ) permutation work qubits, where N is the number of system qubits and S is determined by the support geometry, with S = 1 for nearest-neighbour interactions and S = 3 /2 for all-to-all pairs; in both cases the correction is 

O(log N ). We also introduce a bridged CSWAP, which remembers the logical value of its temporary AND across the target action and repairs it from the prepared labels, so that the inverse CSWAP undoes the exchange without recomputing the value. When the repair is Clifford, it halves the T

count of a matched forward–inverse CSWAP pair at the cost of one retained work qubit. For a Heisenberg ring, the complete block-encoding query reduces the T count by a factor approaching 3 as the system size grows, relative to flat and product-wise Pauli walks. For a five-orbital Anderson impurity model, which combines nearest-neighbour and all-to-all support geometries, the reduction is about 1 .7 at large bath sizes, relative to the cheapest of three comparators, each built from the methods of the references it follows. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Simulating quantum many-body systems has motivated quantum computation since Refs. [1, 2], and it remains among the most promising applications of fault-tolerant machines. In the quantum singular value transformation of Ref. [3], which generalises the quantum signal pro-cessing of Ref. [4], state preparation, time evolution and spectral functions, Refs. [5–9], are all obtained as poly-nomials of a Hamiltonian block-encoded as in Ref. [10], so the non-Clifford cost of the block encoding is paid at every query. In the linear-combination-of-unitaries (LCU) approach of Refs. [11–15], Prepare loads the normalised term weights into a label register and Select applies the unitary labelled there. Generic constructions, such as the unary iteration of Ref. [16], treat the terms as a flat list and pay a T count proportional to their number. The lattice-gauge block encodings of Refs. [17–20] instead iterate over the sites once per term type, so Select is linear in the number of sites for a fixed number of types. Many Hamiltonians of interest have more structure than a list of their terms. A small repertoire of local operators, such as fields, hopping or exchange terms, pla-quette operators and density–density interactions, recurs over many supports arranged by a geometry. Examples are the nearest-neighbour or finite-range bonds of the lat-tice models of Refs. [21, 22], the plaquettes of the lattice gauge theory of Ref. [23], and the all-to-all power-law cou-plings of the Rydberg-atom arrays reviewed in Ref. [24].

Moving selected degrees of freedom to common work po-sitions has precedents in real-space simulation, Ref. [25], fermionic Select , Ref. [26], and first-quantised simula-tion, Refs. [27, 28]. Related chemistry block encodings exchange the two spin sectors of the system register under the control of a single prepared bit, Refs. [29, 30], while Ref. [23] expresses a lattice-gauge block encoding in this form. What differs is the cost that the permutation is built to minimise. Refs. [26–28] select the sites of a term independently, with one swap network per site or register. Ref. [26] builds this network ancilla-free, at logarithmic depth, and Ref. [27] uses it to run the arithmetic once, in ancilla registers. Here, instead, the permutation is built from as few CSWAPs as the structure of the supports allows. This split makes the costs of where and what add in-stead of multiplying. Select circuits whose T count is linear in N already exist, for spin terms, Ref. [31], and for fermionic ones, Ref. [26]. In the product-wise walk of Ref. [31], however, each operator class makes its own pass over the sites, so the coefficient of N grows with the number of classes. Here the two costs have separate sources. The geometry of the supports fixes the permu-tation, the only stage that touches all N system qubits, and thereby how the T count of Select scales with N .The permutation depends on the address alone, so every operator class reuses it. For the Pauli LCUs of this paper, the operator is an XZ mask on T , and further operator classes change only this mask, at a cost independent of N :an explicit mask adds 8 nT T to Prepare however many classes there are. The register-wide mask of Ref. [32] instead spans all N qubits and needs 2 N mask qubits. The number of terms enters the query only through the coefficients that Prepare loads. For the Heisenberg ring, six Pauli terms per site make flat and product-wise walks cost 24 N T gates at leading order, against 8 N for the permutation. Families with different geometries, such as the bath chains and the all-to-all core of an impurity model, combine in one query, Sec. II D. Each keeps its own permutation, and the prepared label makes every inactive family act as the identity, so their costs add. We build the permutation from the CSWAP network of the SelectSwap lookup of Ref. [33]. That lookup swaps classical data words into an output register. Such lookups also enter recent low-T block encodings, Ref. [34], and analyses of the optimal T count of block encodings in the worst case over families of coefficients, Refs. [35, 36]. Our networks move system qubits instead, so the support geometry, not the coefficients, sets their structure. Se-lect then costs 8S N + o(N ) T gates, where the CSWAP density S depends on the geometry alone. It is 1 for finite-range bonds and plaquettes in any dimension, and 3/2 for all-to-all pairs, and in these cases the correction is 

O(log N ). For pair supports, we prove that this CSWAP count is the minimum over address-controlled networks of site transpositions. For supports of any fixed size, any network that delivers every support can be controlled with 

O(log N ) control qubits when Prepare writes one ad-dress per support, but the T count of its controls depends on the form of that address. Delivering the selected qubits and computing the controls that deliver them are thus separate resource problems. For all-to-all pairs, selecting the two sites jointly, instead of with one swap network per site, also lowers the CSWAP count. Replacing each CSWAP by a controlled fermionic transposition permutes fermionic modes at unchanged T count and ancilla count, because the Jordan–Wigner signs it adds need only CZ gates. The matched forward and inverse permutations allow a second saving, in time rather than across terms. Each CSWAP forms a temporary AND of its control and the parity of its two qubits, at 4 T , as reconstructible from Refs. [16, 37, 38]. The inverse CSWAP needs the same bit, and the usual schedule treats it as scratch: it is erased before the target action and recomputed from the system afterwards. The bit can instead be kept across the action. When the prepared labels fix how the action changes the parity of the two qubits, they also fix the update that the kept bit needs, and this repair replaces the recomputation. We call the result a bridged CSWAP. It halves the cost of a matched pair when the repair is Clifford, at the price of one work bit kept live across the action. Permute–act–unpermute, thus, is based on the com-pilation of the permutation from the structure of the Hamiltonian, and a Prepare that writes the address in the form the CSWAP network consumes. We apply this approach to two Hamiltonians to test the two ends of the structure geometry: the Heisenberg ring, with nearest-neighbour bonds, and the Anderson impurity model (AIM), which attaches bath chains to an all-to-all impurity core. As the ring grows, and as the bath grows at a fixed number of impurity orbitals, their complete queries asymptotically need one third of the T

gates of flat and product-wise Pauli walks, and three fifths of those of the cheapest of three comparators, each built from the methods of the references it follows, respectively, at comparable peak workspace. Sec. II builds Select , the bridged CSWAP and the resource law, and Sec. III applies them to the two models. App. A constructs the permutation networks and proves their CSWAP bound, and App. B gives the target actions and the coefficient sampler. Apps. C and D assemble and 3count the complete queries.  

> II. PERMUTE–ACT–UNPERMUTE SELECT

This section constructs a Select for structured Hamil-tonians in which the geometry of the supports sets how its T count scales with N . Further operator classes on the same supports change only the target action, and fam-ilies with different geometries use separate circuits whose costs add. Three ideas achieve this. First, each term is split into where it acts and what it applies. Second, the geometry of the supports fixes the cost of where . Third, a temporary value of the forward permutation is kept and repaired for the inverse permutation, instead of being recomputed. The subsections follow the questions that this construc-tion raises. Sec. II A explains why a flat list of terms is costly. It then splits each term into a permutation and a target action. Counting the resulting cost needs a unit. Sec. II B fixes it as the temporary-AND opening and prices a CSWAP in these units. Sec. II C counts the CSWAPs of the permutation. It shows that the geometry of the supports fixes their number. The CSWAPs and the target action also need controls. Sec. II D specifies what Prepare writes for them and what the remaining control logic costs. Sec. II E combines these pieces into the cost with every CSWAP pair closed. This baseline pays two openings per pair. Sec. II F removes one of them by repairing the work bit of each CSWAP instead of recomputing it. The price is one work bit kept live per pair treated this way.

> C. The support geometry fixes the permutation

The permutation is the only stage that touches every system qubit. Its number of CSWAPs therefore sets the leading cost of Select .The basic building block is a swap tree , the CSWAP network of Ref. [33]. A swap tree acts on M sites, held at its leaves , which are numbered by the address that selects them. With M − 1 CSWAPs, it brings the site at the selected leaf to leaf zero, the root of the tree, so the root is one of the M sites and not an extra qubit. The CSWAPs are arranged in ⌈log 2 M ⌉ layers, one per address bit. Each layer exchanges the roots of pairs of subtrees ,which halves the number of subtree roots at which the selected site can sit. The CSWAPs that act on the roots of the subtrees holding a leaf, one per layer, form the path 

of that leaf, and the selected site travels along the path of its leaf. For M a power of two, the layers thus hold 

M/ 2 + M/ 4 + · · · + 1 = M − 1 CSWAPs. For other M ,the CSWAPs whose upper leaf would lie beyond the M

leaves are omitted, and the count stays M − 1. Fewer CSWAPs cannot suffice. Run backwards from the root, each CSWAP adds at most one site to those that can reach it. Every CSWAP is controlled by a single address bit, as sketched in Fig. 2(d) and drawn in full in Fig. 12. If every support is a single site, T is one qubit and Πj is a single swap tree over all N sites, with that qubit as its root. A two-site support needs one site on each qubit of 

T , so the permutation must combine several trees such that all sites of the selected support arrive together. The support geometry sets how many CSWAPs the permutation needs, and the choice of trees can lower that number. For nearest-neighbour bonds, selecting the two ends of a bond independently uses one tree per end. A first tree over all N sites brings site j to q0. A second tree over all sites brings site j + 1 to q1. Every site then sits in both trees, and Πj holds about 2 N CSWAPs. The bond structure makes half of this work unnecessary. Every bond joins an even site to an odd site. A tree over the even sites alone, rooted at q0, can therefore bring the even end of the bond there. A tree over the odd sites alone, rooted at q1, brings the odd end there. Each site now belongs to a single tree, and the two trees hold N − 2CSWAPs, about half as many as before. The only price is that the two ends of the bond can arrive swapped. Which end lands on q0 now depends on the parity of j. More generally, a network brings the sites of the selected support to T , but which site lands on which qubit of T may depend on the address. The CSWAP counts of this subsection leave that assignment free. An exchange on T controlled by address bits can fix it. Alternatively, the target action can read the low address bits that record where each site landed and act on the right qubits directly, as the Anderson impurity model does in Sec. III B. Either way the extra cost acts on T alone and does not grow with N .The same idea applies whenever the sites split into groups such that no support holds two sites of the same group. Let χ be the least number of such groups. The groups depend only on which sites share a support, not on the coefficients or on a lattice, so the CSWAP counts below hold for any family of supports. Once the groups are fixed, the rest of the structure of the family enters through the controls of these CSWAPs, as discussed below. One tree per group again costs about one CSWAP per site. A second network then moves the tree roots that hold the selected support onto T as in Fig. 2. It is small when χ is bounded, and it becomes the whole permutation at χ = N , as shown below. > D. What Prepare supplies to Select

The permutation and the target action read their con-trols from registers that Prepare writes. The form of these registers decides how much control logic Select 

must still compute. We fix that form by three choices. First, Prepare writes the address in the form that the network reads. A swap tree reads the leaf of the selected site, numbered within the tree of its group, not its index in the system. Converting one into the other may require a few products of address bits. A network that ranges over many tree roots may need further products, such as the retained products of all-to-all pairs, whose number Sec. A 5 establishes at every N . We call all these products 

nonlinear controls and write N pred for their number. Each costs one opening. A control generator G computes them first, and G† erases them at the end. Second, the target action reads the exponents of Eq. (4) from label bits. Prepare can write the label in two forms. A compressed label is as short as the binary logarithm of the number of operator classes. An explicit mask holds all 2nT exponents, which adds 8 nT T to coefficient sampling. Sec. B 1 traces this charge to coherent alias sampling, which spends one opening on each output bit. When each exponent is a label bit or a parity of label bits, it controls one CNOT or one CZ on a target qubit, and 

P is Clifford. An explicit mask always gives this case; the phase of its Y factors is loaded with it, Sec. B 2. A compressed label may also need a few products of label bits, at a cost independent of N . Either way, the cost of 

what does not grow with N .Third, one block encoding can combine several struc-tured families, each with its own permutation, target register and target action. The prepared label flags ex-actly one family in each branch and sets all exponents read by P to zero for the others. Each inactive family then acts as the identity: its target action is trivial, and its permutation and inverse cancel for any address. The fam-ily circuits can therefore run sequentially without outer controls, although their costs add because every branch executes all permutations. This latter construction resem-bles the one of Ref. [40]. The Anderson impurity model of Sec. III B combines its bath chains and all-to-all impurity core in this way.

XX , Y Y , Z and ZZ without strings. In the all-to-all net-work, the strings of each tree layer span about N modes, so one CZ per mode would cost O(N log N ) Clifford gates. Each tree CSWAP acts after both of its subtrees have finished, so the modes between its two endpoints are the finished lower subtree and idle modes of the other tree. > B. Anderson impurity model

The Anderson impurity model (AIM) describes inter-acting orbitals coupled to a non-interacting bath, Ref. [60]. It is the auxiliary problem of dynamical mean-field theory, which maps a correlated lattice onto an impurity with a self-consistent bath, Refs. [61, 62]. For real materials, the lattice model is itself derived from first principles, with localised orbitals and screened interactions, Refs. [63, 64]. Accurate bath representations require many sites, beyond the reach of exact diagonalisation. The AIM has there-fore been proposed as the quantum step of a classical embedding loop, Refs. [65, 66]. Tensor-network solvers can also treat it efficiently, Ref. [67], but rapid entangle-ment growth during real time evolution, particularly in multi-orbital systems, makes these problems promising candidates for quantum computing, Refs. [8, 68, 69]. The AIM combines the chain and all-to-all geometries of Tab. I in one Hamiltonian. Its one-body terms live on long nearest-neighbour chains. Its interactions, by contrast, couple every pair of impurity orbitals. Tab. II sets the AIM beside the ring, step by step. Its one-body family is the ring on every chain at once, plus one stage that picks the chain. Its interaction family is an all-to-all network on the impurity orbitals. > IV. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have shown how the repeated structure of a Hamiltonian can be compiled into a block encoding whose Select has a T count set by the geometry of its supports. Writing each term as a local operator con-jugated by a support-dependent permutation separates 

where it acts from what it applies, so that the two costs add instead of multiplying. This separation has the prece-dents discussed in Sec. I, and Select circuits whose T

count is linear in N already exist for spin terms, Ref. [31], for fermionic terms, Ref. [26], and for particular term types, Refs. [16, 71, 72]. What we add is a systematic way to make the separation cheap. A CSWAP network fixed by the support geometry brings the selected support to the target register, an XZ mask on that register alone applies the operator, and Prepare writes the address in the form the network consumes. The geometry of the supports then sets how the T count of Select scales with N . The permutation depends on the address alone, so every operator class reuses it, and further operator classes change only the XZ mask, at a cost that does not grow with N . Families with different geometries, such as the bath chains and the all-to-all core of the impurity model, combine in one query, and their costs add. Against constructions that are already linear, the gain is at most a constant factor. For the Heisenberg ring, the leading cost is 8 N T gates, against 24 N for flat and product-wise Pauli walks. For all-to-all pairs, it is the same 12 N as the plane-wave chemistry construction of Ref. [16] but for any Pauli operator on the pair, and, since the Jordan–Wigner signs of a controlled fermionic transposition are Clifford, four times fewer T gates than the ancilla-free quadratic fermionic circuit of Ref. [26]. The cost of selecting where a term acts is determined by the connectivity of the Hamiltonian. This connectivity fixes the CSWAP network that brings the selected sup-port to a fixed target register. For two-qubit supports, the resulting Select costs 8S N + o(N ) T gates with 

O(log N ) ancillae, where S depends only on the connec-tivity. It ranges from S = 1 for nearest-neighbour and bounded-range connectivity to S = 3 /2 for all-to-all con-nectivity, and at both ends the correction is O(log N ). In particular, the leading cost of Select is independent of the number and values of the coupling coefficients, so power-law couplings cost the same there as uniform ones; the coefficients enter through Prepare . We also proved that the CSWAP count is minimal within this class of networks, Sec. A 2 b. When every set of three sites is a support, the density is again S = 3 /2, but the mini-mum exceeds 3 N/ 2 by a term of order √N , Sec. A 2 c. For supports of any fixed size, any network that delivers all supports can be controlled with logarithmic control workspace when Prepare writes one address per support, but the T count of its controls depends on the form of that address, Sec. A 3 d. For bounded connectivity, part of the saving comes from how the address and operator label are represented, suggesting that the same principle may benefit other selection mechanisms. The matched forward and inverse permutations provide a second saving. Instead of erasing a temporary AND and recomputing it for the inverse permutation, a bridged CSWAP remembers its logical value across the target ac-tion and repairs it using the prepared labels. When the repair is Clifford, this halves the T cost of a matched pair at the price of one additional ancilla. The same applies to any temporary value whose inputs an intervening oper-ation changes in a way fixed by registers that the circuit already holds. In the examples studied here, however, bridging affects only subleading terms. The dominant saving comes from a permutation that every operator class reuses, where a walk over the Pauli list pays for each site once per class. We also adapted Prepare to the information that Se-lect consumes and compared complete block-encoding queries. For the Heisenberg ring, the resulting query uses about three times fewer T gates than flat and product-wise Pauli walks. For the Anderson impurity model, which combines long bath chains with an all-to-all interacting core, it uses about 1 .7 times fewer T gates than the cheap-est of three comparators, each built from the methods of the references it follows. These reductions are obtained at comparable peak workspace and at the same block-encoding normalisation, to within 0 .3% for the sparse AIM comparator, so an algorithm built on these block encodings makes essentially the same number of queries, each at the reduced cost. Several questions remain open. For general families of supports of three or more sites, the minimum CSWAP count is known in closed form only for χ = nT , Sec. A 2 b. Its leading order is N whenever χ grows more slowly than N , and 3 N/ 2 for the complete family of three-site supports, Eq. (A4). For other families whose χ grows in proportion to N , including the complete families of four or more sites, the leading order remains open. The nonlinear control cost remains O(log N ) for supports of fixed size, Sec. A 3 c, and a minimum-size network can always be controlled with O(log N ) control qubits when Prepare 

writes one address per support, but whether some address encoding also keeps its control openings O(log N ) is open. Since every operator class on a support reuses the same permutation, the margin over walks that pay once per class grows with the number of classes, so models with many operator classes per support, such as multi-orbital lattice models, are natural next targets. More broadly, these results suggest compiling the geometry of a Hamilto-nian into the permutation that selects its supports, which then sets the leading cost, and its repeated local operators into a target action that every support shares.

1. Gate cost and work-bit reuse 

A temporary AND closes for free when the stored bit still equals the product of its current controls. In the circuits, a stored product begins one column after its opening. Whether the work bit of a CSWAP can be reused for its inverse depends on how the action between them changes its inputs. 

a. Keeping the work bit across the action 

For an isolated matched pair, suppose the target action changes the pair parity by δ. The inverse gate needs 

ω ⊕ cδ , so applying Xcδ ω repairs the stored bit and allows a free close, Eq. (8). This requires δ to depend only on the prepared address and label. Pauli actions satisfy this condition: their X exponents determine the bit flips, while their Z factors leave the parity unchanged. An action that superposes parity sectors has no such fixed δ

and uses the closed schedule. A diagonal action needs no repair. An affine repair predicate cδ is applied by CNOTs, so the pair again pays only one opening. A product predicate needs an opening of its own, so an isolated pair then costs two openings, as a closed one does; bridging saves only when several work bits share that product. A bridge that saves an opening saves 4 T and retains one work bit, as counted in Eq. (9). Sec. A 6 extends this rule to a network.

> b. Support geometry fixes the CSWAP count

Let each support contain nT of the N sites, with every site in at least one support. At each level, the control generator reads the two candidate bins of each selected site from its code and chooses distinct ones by a fixed number of label comparisons. The edge layer reads one affine Boolean function of the edge label. Such a function can take any prescribed values on three distinct labels, because their two differences from the first are distinct and nonzero, hence linearly independent over 

F2, and elimination on their lowest nonzero bits finds its coefficients with O(log N ) openings. A tree layer cannot use one such function, because every occupied bin needs its own leaf, and a separate function for each bit of the leaf would cost O(log 2 N ) openings. Instead, the labels are read as elements of a binary field whose degree is a power of two, and the tree of every bin reads the bits of one polynomial of degree two at the label of that bin, the polynomial that takes the leaf of each selected site at the label of its bin. Multiplication by a fixed label is linear over F2, so every enable is a parity of the three stored coefficients. These follow from the three bins and leaves by eleven multiplications and one inversion in the field, and the inversion reduces, through the norm to the subfield of half the degree, to an inversion there and three multiplications. A bilinear multiplication algorithm spends one opening for each of its products of two linear forms, and over F2 there are such algorithms with a number of products linear in the degree of the field [77]. > 3. Address controls

The transposition bound excludes the cost of its con-trols. A swap tree reads single address bits and needs no further products. Address conversion and selection among tree roots can require nonlinear controls, counted by N pred in Eq. (7). The control generator computes and retains the products that the chosen permutation network needs. A toggle is needed only when consecutive trees share a field but read different addresses.  

> a. Control generators

The control generator G computes products of prepared bits before the first permutation, and G† erases them after the inverse permutation. They close by measurement once their inputs have been restored. Products include toggle carries or borrows and network controls, counted by N pred ,and repair predicates, counted separately by N rep . This separates control generation from the order of the system permutations: an all-to-all network reads the controls directly, whereas trees that share an address field use 

Tog between stages to change the field and later restore it. A product needed in only one stage may instead be computed around that stage in each direction. This adds one opening but can reduce peak workspace, as in Sec. A 5. The trees read one position field per group, so Prepare 

supplies an address of up to χ⌈log 2 N ⌉ bits. That address is charged to Prepare , while the CSWAP count sets the leading term of Select . A network on a bounded number of tree roots contributes a bounded number of controls. A logarithmic count arises in two places. The first is where the prepared address must be converted into the fields that the trees read, as for the toggle of Sec. A 3 b. The second is where the network ranges over all sites, as for the retained products of all-to-all pairs, Sec. A 5.

> d. Controls for any network

Any network that delivers every support, a minimum one included, can be controlled coherently from any pre-pared encoding of its naddr ≥ 2 valid addresses. > 6. Repairs inside a network

For a bridged gate λ inside a network, the target action is carried back through the intervening CSWAPs. Its parity change δλ(j, b ) is the XOR of the X-mask bits that reach the two positions exchanged by λ. The Z

enables the gate. Affine predicates require only CNOTs; nonlinear predicates are built from products and shared wherever possible. Their openings contribute N rep in Eq. (9). A diagonal action needs no repairs. In a swap tree, a bridged CSWAP sees a parity change only if it lies on the path of the leaf whose block the action flips. A compressed label uses only enough bits to distinguish the operator classes, but may require decoding in the target action. Its cost T act depends on the repertoire. > 3. The coefficient sampler

Prepare must give each term an amplitude set by its coefficient. One amplitude per term would add a lookup linear in the number of terms, which grows as the number of supports times the number of operator classes. Terms that share a magnitude, however, need it loaded only once, so the lookup grows only with the number of distinct coefficient rows, constant for the ring and linear in the bath for the AIM, and preparation alias-samples the distinct coefficient rows and uniform superpositions then pick one of the equal-weight terms of the sampled row. Loading only the distinct coefficients and spreading them over equivalent terms is standard. Ref. [16] does so for the Hubbard and plane-wave Hamiltonians, and Ref. [71] for the symmetries of the two-electron integrals. The signs are loaded with the magnitudes and applied by the diagonal of Sec. B 2. The coherent alias sampler of Ref. [16] loads an al-ternate row and a μ-bit keep threshold. A comparison with a uniform μ-bit coin sets the keep flag, which con-trols one CSWAP per loaded bit. These gates cause the mask charge above. Since Select leaves the label un-changed, their work bits remain valid through the query and close without repair during inverse preparation. The alias workspace stays live until then. The form in which the sampler writes the address also fixes the cost of the controls. Any network can be con-trolled from any encoding, but at an opening count that grows with the number of supports, Sec. A 3 d. The leading cost comes from the one-body trees and coefficient loading. Bridging reduces the chain-front and interaction costs while retaining work bits. Both target actions and all four sign factors are Clifford. The row-address and rank superpositions supply the rotations; we count them separately from exact T gates.

> 6. Comparison

We compare one uncontrolled complete query, compris-ing Select and both directions of Prepare , at the same model size, coefficient precision μ and normalisation. All counts include the rotation-synthesis estimate of Eq. (C10) at εsynth = 10 −3. Workspace excludes the system register. Fig. 10 compares three constructions with ours. 

a. Comparator constructions. The flat comparator applies unary iteration from Ref. [16] to the full list of 

L = 3 N + 4 n2imp − 7nimp non-identity Pauli strings. The 

structured comparator uses the coefficient factoring and indexed selection of that reference, with the same R rows as Eq. (D3). The coefficients do not depend on spin, hop-ping axis or orbital pair, so uniform refinements supply these choices. A uniform spin bit controls CSWAPs that substitute the spin-down copies of the selection fields. A uniform coin controls a CSWAP of the two hopping indices, and its Select applies Y Y when they arrive in decreasing order and XX otherwise. The orbital pair is a uniform a with b = a + e + 1 mod nimp , for an offset e

uniform over the other nimp − 1 orbitals. Its one-body se-lection uses two selected-Majorana passes and an indexed 

Z. Hund exchange and pair hopping lie outside the four term types that this selection admits. Each factors into one two-qubit Pauli per orbital, selected by additional impurity passes. In total, the comparator uses 3 N − 537 one-body openings and six passes of nimp − 1 openings. The one-body block also exchanges its two nN -bit index registers under a single control bit and later reverses the exchange, with nN = ⌈log 2 N ⌉; each direction costs one opening per exchanged qubit pair. The comparator Se-lect thus costs 4(3 N + 6 nimp + 2 nN − 11) raw T . No pass visits all orbital pairs. The sparse comparator follows Refs. [29, 71], tabling the d = N +nimp (nimp −1) nonzero integrals of the sparse (pq |rs ) representation. Its Select applies the Majorana pair twice, costing about 16 N rather than the leading 12 N of the structured comparator. Measured lookup erasure offsets this extra selection cost. The structured comparator instead reverses its lookup at full cost, as in Ref. [16], which predates the measured erasure of Ref. [71]. Each comparator is built only from the methods of the references it follows, without the constructions of this paper. Every CSWAP of a comparator, including those of its alias sampler, is charged one opening per exchanged qubit pair, as in this paper. 

b. Coefficient loading. Our preparation and the struc-tured comparator alias-sample the R coefficient rows of Eq. (D3), the onsite and hopping coefficients of H1b , and the few parameters of Hint . The sparse comparator alias-samples its d nonzero integrals, and the flat comparator one amplitude per Pauli string. The flat lookup cost depends on which entries of its alias table repeat, and hence on the coefficient values, so it is counted at every plotted size rather than bounded. The structured lookup loads row fields, alternates and the keep word together. It also loads spin-down copies of the selection fields to implement the snake ordering, adding workspace. 

c. Cost and workspace. As the bath grows at fixed orbital count, our complete query asymptotically needs three fifths of the T gates of the cheapest comparator, 40% fewer. This is the ratio of the leading terms in Fig. 10(c), 24 nimp nbath here against 40 nimp nbath for the sparse and structured comparators. In the same spirit as the structured comparator the measured erasure of Ref. [71], it is possible to build a variant that lowers its leading term to 32 nimp nbath , so the saving becomes one quarter; the flat comparator, improved in the same way, remains the most expensive. All four costs are linear in bath size at fixed orbital count, so the reduction is a constant factor. At five orbitals, the cheapest comparator is sparse at bath size 100 and structured at 1000, with the two costs agreeing to within two per cent. The saving is larger at small baths, where subleading terms matter, and our construction costs less at every plotted size. At five orbitals and bath sizes 100 and 1000, our query needs just over a third of the T gates of the flat comparator. Rotation counts remain bounded as the model grows: six here, four for flat and sparse, and eight for structured. Panel (c) omits these bounded synthesis costs. Panel (b) shows the workspace tradeoff. At every plot-ted size, our peak is set by the one-body chain-front permutation, with its bridged lanes and repair word live. At five orbitals and bath sizes 100 and 1000, our peak lies within seven per cent of the sparse comparator’s, an eighth to a fifth below the structured comparator’s, and about twice the flat comparator’s. The reduction in T

count does not imply a uniform reduction in workspace.
