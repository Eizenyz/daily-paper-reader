Dissipative state preparation engineers quantum many-body states as steady states of an open-system dynamics. The spatial range of the required dissipation sets both the feasibility of analog implementations and the connectivity and control complexity of digital ones. Yet how this locality quantitatively constrains preparation times remains largely unknown. Here, we establish an exact locality-preparation time relation for quadratic fermionic systems and develop a construction that optimizes the locality of the dissipative dynamics while preserving the target state. Applied to the Kitaev chain, our framework reveals sharply different locality-preparation time tradeoffs in critical and gapped regimes. At criticality, suppressing the algebraic tails of the jump operators necessarily forces the preparation time to grow algebraically with system size. By contrast, both trivial and topological gapped phases admit exponentially localized dissipation with a finite preparation time in the thermodynamic limit. Our results identify when fast dissipative preparation is compatible with local control and when criticality imposes an unavoidable dynamical cost. 

Engineering complex quantum states in many-body systems is a central challenge in quantum computation and simulation [1–4]. Existing approaches include adi-abatic [5] and quench [6] protocols, variational [7] and tensor-network-inspired methods [8], and measurement-or feedback-assisted schemes [9, 10]. Dissipative state preparation (DSP) provides a complementary strategy, in which engineered system-environment interactions make the target state an attractive fixed point of the dynam-ics [11–21]. This fixed-point structure can reduce sensi-tivity to initialization and provide resilience against im-perfections [22]. A central question in DSP concerns the fundamental limits on preparation times. Local Lindbladians obey Lieb-Robinson bounds [23], which constrain the propa-gation of information and therefore the build-up of long-range correlations. Moreover, it has been shown that local gapped Lindbladians feature exponential decay of correlations in the steady state [24]. Complementarily, recent work has established a fundamental tradeoff be-tween steady-state entanglement and relaxation time un-der bipartite locality constraints [25]. Together, these re-sults show that locality limits DSP, but leave open how the preparation time depends on the spatial range of the jump operators. Quantifying this dependence is essential for scalable implementations, where the jump-operator range sets the required connectivity and control complex-ity. One-dimensional topological superconductors provide a minimal setting in which this question can be addressed across different phases. Their topological phase hosts Majorana boundary modes and supports nonlocal infor-mation encoding [26, 27]. These systems can also host critical states at continuous transitions between phases, characterized by correlations that decay algebraically. These features have established them as central targets of many-body physics [28, 29] and quantum simulation [30–37]. Criti-cal states can also exhibit metrologically useful properties that remain robust against different sources of decoher-ence, highlighting their potential as promising resources for quantum technologies [38, 39]. At the same time, their divergent correlation length makes them especially challenging to prepare [40]. We use the Kitaev chain to address a central question: does criticality or topology impose a fundamental tradeoff between dissipator local-ity and preparation time? Here, we answer this question exactly for quadratic dis-sipative systems. The preparation strategy is illustrated in Fig. 1(a): the system is driven toward the ground state by dissipating quasiparticles. We exploit the freedom to mix Bogoliubov annihilation operators without changing the target state, constructing jump operators that are optimally localized in real space and determining the as-sociated preparation-time cost. At criticality, faster alge-braic decay of the jump-operator tails comes at the cost of a larger exponent governing the polynomial growth of preparation time with system size. By contrast, in both trivial and topological gapped phases, exponentially lo-calized jump operators can coexist with a finite prepara-tion time in the thermodynamic limit. Moreover, along a special curve in the topological phase, the optimal jump operators become strictly finite-range.

In practice, however, the available dissipative couplings will generally be restricted to a finite spatial range. It is therefore crucial to determine how truncating the tails of the jump operators affects the accuracy of the pre-pared steady state, both at the level of physical ob-servables and of the global state. In Fig. 3, we impose the same finite-range cutoff on the three representative points highlighted in Fig. 2 and compare the resulting steady-state correlations with those of the exact target ground state. At the critical points, directly truncating the real-space Bogoliubov annihilators ( n = 0) produces sizable deviations. By contrast, for n > 0 we first ex-ploit the nontrivial mixing βk = | cos k|n at XX criticality and βk = | cos( k/ 2) |n at Ising criticality, which suppress the momentum-space singularities and thereby the alge-braic real-space tails before applying the same trunca-tion. This substantially improves the agreement at fixed spatial range. In the gapped topological phase, where the jump operators are exponentially localized, direct trun-cation remains more accurate. The inset quantifies the corresponding global error through the steady-state infi-delity, showing that an appropriate choice of the suppres-sion exponent n can strongly improve the fidelity of the finite-range dissipator. Note that the fidelity curve shows a non-monotonicity with the tradeoff exponent, which originates from the interplay between real-space trunca-tion and strong suppression of the momentum prefactors close to the point of discontinuity. While the jump oper-ator coefficients close to the momentum discontinuity are very small for large n, the error through truncation can remain comparably large. As for the fidelity the ratio of prefactors matters, the truncation errors can get strongly amplified (see SM). 

Discussion and outlook– We have developed a gen-eral framework for optimizing the locality of dissipative ground-state preparation in quadratic fermionic systems, exploiting the freedom to mix Bogoliubov annihilation operators without changing the target state. The re-sulting exact locality-preparation time relation is con-trolled by the momentum-space regularity of the Bogoli-ubov coefficients: at criticality, the more strongly the algebraic tails of the jump operators are suppressed, the more severely the preparation time grows (polynomially) with system size. These results establish dissipator locality as a quantitative resource for scalable DSP, link-ing the required interaction range directly to the achiev-able preparation time. The odd- and even-parity character of the low-lying Li-ouvillian modes also leads to an interesting initial-state dependence of the relaxation dynamics. While fermion-parity superselection excludes the slowest mode for phys-ical fermionic states, these modes become accessible in spin realizations. As shown in the SM, eliminating their initial overlap yields a twofold Mpemba-type preparation speedup [46–48]. Looking ahead, an important direction is to investigate whether measurements, feedback, and adaptive open-system dynamics [49] can mitigate or circumvent the locality-preparation time tradeoffs derived here. More-over, it would be valuable to explore how these relations are modified by non-Markovian and spectrally structured reservoirs [50], where memory effects and frequency selec-tivity provide additional resources for state preparation. 5Extending the analysis beyond quadratic systems repre-sents a further central challenge. Interacting many-body models could be studied numerically using methods such as CLIK-MPS [51], which enables the computation of dis-sipative gaps in large-scale systems. Finally, translating the optimized dissipators into experimental protocols will require accounting for restrictions on available couplings and reservoir engineering in platforms such as ultracold atoms [52, 53], trapped ions [54], and superconducting-qubit processors [55].

Appendix E: Mpemba-type accelerated state preparation 

As discussed in the main text and in App. B, the slowest Lindbladian eigenmodes correspond to coherences between states of opposite fermion parity and are therefore odd under fermion-parity conjugation. Such coherences are excluded from physical fermionic density matrices by fermion-parity superselection [8, 9]. Consequently, physical fermionic states have vanishing overlap with these modes, and their asymptotic relaxation is governed by the next decay rate, 2∆ L. This can be understood directly from the spectral decomposition ˆρ(t) − ˆρss = X

> α

cαeλαt ˆRα, (E1) where ˆRα are right Lindbladian eigenmodes and the coefficients cα are fixed by the initial state. The vanishing of the coefficient associated with the slowest Lindbladian mode is precisely the spectral mechanism underlying a strong Mpemba effect [10–12]: the asymptotic relaxation is then controlled by a faster mode. Figure 2 illustrates this mechanism explicitly at a representative point on the XX critical line (for the phase diagram, see Fig. 2 in the main text). The coherent superposition of states with opposite fermion parity contains coherences between the corresponding parity sectors and therefore accesses the slowest odd-parity Lindbladian modes, relaxing asymptotically at the rate ∆ L. By contrast, the two definite-parity initial states have vanishing overlap with these modes and consequently relax faster, at the rate 2∆ L. While these parity coherences are forbidden for physical fermionic density matrices, they are allowed in spin or hard-core-boson realizations, where no analogous fermion-parity superselection rule applies. In such systems, the spectral structure therefore identifies initial states with vanishing overlap with the slowest modes and hence provides a strong-Mpemba-type factor-of-two acceleration of dissipative state preparation [13, 14].
