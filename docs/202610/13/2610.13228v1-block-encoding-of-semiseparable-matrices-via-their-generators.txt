BLOCK ENCODING OF SEMISEPARABLE MATRICES VIA THEIR GENERATORS ∗

GIACOMO ANTONIOLI † , GIANNA M. DEL CORSO ‡ , AND ROEL VAN BEEUMEN §

Abstract. We present an explicit quantum circuit that constructs a block encoding of a generator-representable semiseparable (one-pair) matrix directly from its two generators. For an 

N × N semiseparable matrix with N = 2n, the circuit uses 2 n + 2 ancilla qubits and has O(polylog N )

depth, and it realizes a (2√N , 2n + 2, 2√2 ε)-block encoding, where ε is controlled by the accuracy of the state-preparation routines that load the generators. The building blocks of the circuit are cyclic shifts, swaps, and Hadamard gates, each admitting an efficient implementation through the binary decomposition of the relevant indices. 

Key words. block encoding, semiseparable matrices, quantum linear algebra, quantum circuits

1. Introduction. Block encodings are the standard input model for quantum linear algebra. Indeed, quantum algorithms for solving linear systems, estimating spectral quantities, or applying matrix functions all assume access to a unitary whose leading block is a suitably scaled copy of the target matrix, and their efficiency is in-herited from the quality of that encoding, namely its ancilla count, its circuit depth, and the subnormalization factor by which the matrix is scaled. Since most algorithms simply presuppose such access, explicit and economical constructions have drawn increasing attention. Structured matrices have long been a source of algorithmic gains in classical numerical linear algebra: sparsity patterns, displacement structure, banded-ness and off-diagonal low-rank structure each translate into fast solvers, and it is natural to ask whether the same structure yields analogous savings on a quan-tum computer. For sparse matrices Camps et al. [5] give circuits acting directly on the sparsity pattern, while FABLE [6] targets arbitrary dense matrices through an approximate, compressible circuit. For matrices with displacement structure this is by now well established. Effi-cient quantum circuits and block encodings have been designed for circulant, Toeplitz and Hankel matrices by exploiting their parametrization through a small number of generators and their close relationship with the quantum Fourier transform [19, 31]; displacement-structured matrices have been decomposed into linear combinations of shift matrices to yield ε-approximate block encodings in both black-box and QRAM data-access models [30], and more recent work builds block encodings directly from an arithmetic description of a matrix’s sparsity and repeated-value pattern, with

For rank-structured matrices, Nguyen, Kiani and Lloyd [23] block-encode hierarchical (HODLR/HSS) kernel matrices by re-cursively combining encodings of their low-rank blocks through linear combinations of unitaries, attaining near-optimal linear-system runtimes for dense, full-rank kernels, and Tang and Lai [26] obtain a direct hierarchical encoding that lowers the required oracle access from O(N 2) to O(N ) entries. The common thread across these results is that it is data sparsity, i.e., the number of parameters needed to describe the matrix, rather than the number of its nonzero entries that governs the achievable quantum cost, exactly as it governs classical complexity. Generator-representable semiseparable matrices are a particularly clean instance of this principle: the entire N × N matrix is fixed by two length-N vectors, giving a data-sparse yet dense, full-rank object. Beyond their defining role as inverses of irre-ducible tridiagonal matrices, they arise as the Green’s functions of two-point bound-ary value problems, as covariance matrices of Gauss–Markov and Brownian-motion type processes in statistics and filtering, in the oscillation theory of small mechanical vibrations, and in the theory of orthogonal polynomials [12, 29]. A first quantum block encoding tailored to this class was recently proposed in [1], where S is encoded through a triangular-diagonal factorization; the resulting circuit has polylogarithmic depth, but the factorization is paid for by a subnormalization factor and an error that both grow as O(N 2). In this work we show that no factorization is needed. Loading the two generators into separate registers already produces every product uivj , hence every entry of S(u, v ), and an explicit circuit of cyclic shifts, swaps and Hadamard gates discards the spurious products and routes each entry to its place in the leading block. The resulting encoding has O(polylog N ) depth and uses 2 n + 2 ancillas in-stead of 2 n + 7, while the subnormalization drops from O(N 2) to 2 √N and the error from O(N 2ε) to 2 √2 ε, independently of N . Its only matrix-dependent ingredients are the two state preparations, and it extends to the unsymmetric case. A detailed comparison is given in Section 7. The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the notation and preliminary facts about quantum computation. In Section 3 we review the definition of generator representable semiseparable matrices, in both the symmetric and unsym-metric case. Section 4 introduces the building blocks of our construction, namely the cyclic shift and the composite operators derived from it, together with their circuits. Section 5 is devoted to the main result of this paper, the block encoding of symmetric, generator representable semiseparable matrices: there we present the main idea and the complete circuit, and then assemble the building blocks to prove the theorem. Sec-tion 6 extends the result to unsymmetric generator-representable matrices. Section 7 compares our block encoding with the state of the art for relevant classes of struc-tured matrices. Numerical experiments are presented in Section 8, and conclusions in Section 9.

Multi-controlled gates extend this idea by requiring several control qubits to be in a particular configuration before the target unitary is applied: on a state vector, only the component corresponding to that configuration is transformed by U , while as an operator the gate is block-diagonal, acting as U on the block selected by the configuration and as the identity on all remaining blocks. a, b and is controlled by the state of the qubit c.A quantum circuit is simply an ordered sequence of gates of this kind. 

Block encodings. In quantum computing all the valid operations require the op-erators to be unitary matrices. In classical numerical linear algebra many problems tackle general matrices, that do not respect this requirement. In order to extend the usage of quantum computing to all the linear algebra problems, we need to be able to represent non unitary matrices in these systems. 3. Semiseparable Matrices. A matrix is called semiseparable when every sub-matrix extracted from its lower triangular part, and every submatrix extracted from its upper triangular part, has rank at most one, so that its off-diagonal structure is entirely low-rank [11, 29]. Matrices of this kind, and the broader family of rank-structured matrices to which they belong, arise frequently in applications and have long been exploited to design fast algorithms for solving linear systems, computing eigenvalues, and other fundamental tasks in numerical linear algebra [29, 11, 3, 8, 20]. In this paper we focus on the subclass in which the rank-one off-diagonal structure is described globally by two vectors: the generator-representable, or one-pair, semisep-arable matrices, originally introduced in [12] in the context of operator theory.

The duality between tridiagonal and generator-representable semiseparable matri-ces [24] admits an interesting generalization. A fundamental result due to Asplund [2] establishes that the inverse of a nonsingular band matrix of bandwidth p is semisep-arable of order p, in the sense that every submatrix lying entirely above or below the main diagonal has rank at most p. Equivalently, this can be expressed via the nullity theorem [28]: for an invertible matrix A, the rank of any subblock of A−1 is related to the nullity of a complementary subblock of A, providing a precise quantitative link between the sparsity structure of a matrix and the off-diagonal rank structure of its inverse. These results were developed and extended to the block and nonsymmetric settings by several authors [9, 10], while the structural properties of semiseparable matrices, including uniqueness of the generator representation and behavior under unitary similarity transformations, are studied in [4]. To place the class treated in this paper within the broader landscape of rank-structured matrices, we recall the relevant hierarchy. A matrix is quasiseparable (of order one) when the rank-at-most-one condition is imposed only on its strictly trian-gular submatrices, leaving the diagonal free; adding a free diagonal to a semiseparable matrix yields the semiseparable-plus-diagonal matrices, which are quasiseparable but not, in general, semiseparable. At the top of the hierarchy, HODLR (hierarchically off-diagonal low-rank) and HSS (hierarchically semiseparable) matrices [20] relax the single-level, rank-one condition to a bounded off-diagonal rank imposed recursively across a hierarchical partition of the index set. One thus has the strict inclusions (3.1) {gen.-repr. } ⊊ { semisep. } ⊊ { semisep. + diag. } ⊊ { quasisep. } ⊊ { HODLR/HSS }.

The first inclusion is strict because a diagonal matrix with nonzero entries is semisep-arable but not generator-representable; the third because an irreducible tridiagonal matrix is quasiseparable yet not semiseparable-plus-diagonal. The construction of this paper targets the smallest class in (3.1), and Section 9 discusses its extension to the larger ones.

6. The unsymmetric case. The construction extends to general (non-symme-tric) generator-representable semiseparable matrices at the sole cost of making the state preparation controlled and of replacing the Hadamard gate on the triangular qubit by a suitable rotation. Before stating the result we discuss why the unit-norm normalization of Theorem 5.1 cannot be carried over unchanged. A one-pair matrix S(u, v, x, y ) of Definition 3.3 is determined by four generators subject to the diagonal compatibility uivi = xiyi, i = 0, . . . , N −1, which makes the two triangular expressions agree on the diagonal. Requiring in addition that all four gen-erators be unit-norm is an overdetermined constraint: normalizing u, v, x, y indepen-dently rescales the two triangular parts by the different factors ∥u∥2∥v∥2 and ∥x∥2∥y∥2,and the compatibility is destroyed unless these two products happen to coincide. In other words, one cannot in general prescribe both ∥u∥2 = ∥ v∥2 = ∥ x∥2 = ∥ y∥2 = 1 and 

uivi = xiyi.We therefore drop the normalization hypothesis altogether and block-encode S =

S(u, v, x, y ) for arbitrary generators. This is possible because the two triangular parts are separately bilinear in their own pair of generators. Writing L(u, v ) for the lower triangular part including the diagonal, Lij = uj vi for i ≥ j, and R(x, y ) for the strictly upper one, Rij = xiyj for i < j, we have S = L(u, v ) + R(x, y ) and, denoting by

7. Comparison with other encoding. In this section we compare our con-struction with existing block-encoding schemes that are applicable to generator-repre-sentable semiseparable matrices, either because they are tailored to (a superclass of) this structure, or because they apply to arbitrary dense matrices. The relevant pa-rameters for the comparison are the subnormalization factor α, the number of ancilla qubits, the error of the final encoding, the circuit depth or gate count, and the data-access model, that is, the form in which the matrix must be supplied to the circuit: state-preparation routines for the generators, oracle access to matrix entries, or clas-sical data compiled directly into the circuit. The scheme of [1] is, to the best of our knowledge, the only previous block encoding tailored to one-pair matrices, and is therefore the closest term of comparison. It is based on a triangular-diagonal factorization of S, and while it achieves polylogarithmic depth, the factorization is paid for by a subnormalization factor growing as O(N 2),together with 2 log N + 7 ancillas. Our construction bypasses any factorization and works with the generators alone: the only matrix-dependent ingredients are the two state-preparation routines, no classical pre-processing of the matrix is required, and the subnormalization drops from O(N 2) to 2 √N . The improvement in α is not a detail of the encoding but propagates through any subsequent algorithm: in quantum singular value transformation and related solvers, the cost of applying a function of the encoded matrix scales with α (e.g., as O(ακ polylog (1/ϵ)) for linear systems with condition number κ [13, 7]), so the reduction from N 2 to √N yields a polynomial saving on the whole pipeline. We remark that α = 2√N is not optimal in absolute terms: any (α, m, ε )-block-encoding requires α ≥ ∥ S∥2, and for unit-norm generators ∥S∥2 ≤ ∥ S∥F ≤ √2, so the overhead α/∥ S∥2 can be as large as Θ (√N ). The factor √N originates from the final Hadamard layer that superposes the N column copies, and is analogous to the normalization loss incurred by FABLE-type constructions. Whether it can be removed within a generator-based, factorization-free construction is an open question. In Figure 7.1 we plot the ratio r(N ) = α/∥ S∥2 for semiseparable matrices whose generators are constructed in three different ways (see Section 8 for the details). For 

α = 2√N we see the overhead for our method while for α = N we see the overhead of FABLE. The comparison in terms of the error in the encoding shows another improvement of the algorithm of this paper over [1]. The error in [1] grows as O(N 2ε), where here is 2 √2ε, independent on the size of the encoded matrix. Semiseparable matrices are the simplest instance of the hierarchical rank-struc-tured classes addressed in [23, 26], so the natural term of comparison is the block encoding of general H-matrices of [23, Section 4.3]. In this framework, S(u, v ) is split by recursive bisection of the index set into O(N ) off-diagonal blocks distributed over log N levels; an interesting fact is that every such block in the semiseparable case is exactly of rank one, being the outer product of two contiguous portions of the generators. The scheme of [23] block-encodes each block from state preparations of the normalized restrictions of generators, assembles each level as a block-sparse matrix, and sums the levels by a linear combination of unitaries. For unit-norm generators the resulting subnormalization is O(log N ), asymptotically smaller than our 2 √N ; the gain, however, is paid in the access model, which requires state-preparation oracles multiplexed over all blocks of the hierarchy together with an oracle for the O(N )

partial norms of the portions of the generators, i.e., classically precomputed data of the same size as the generators themselves. Moreover, the input inaccuracy of these oracles is amplified by the subnormalization and accumulated across the levels. BLOCK ENCODING OF SEMISEPARABLE MATRICES 21 Our construction is different; it requires nothing beyond two plain state preparations of the full generators, involves no classical pre-processing, no multiplexing, and no propagation of errors, at the price of a larger subnormalization. The direct scheme of [26] for hierarchically block separable matrices lowers the oracle access to O(N )

entries, but builds the encoding recursively by multiplying per-level block encodings, so that both the subnormalization and the error propagate multiplicatively along the 

O(log N ) levels of the recursion, yielding bounds that grow polynomially with N on this class; their alternative sparsification approach achieves constant subnormalization but encodes an extended sparse system rather than S itself, and is a tool for linear solves rather than a block encoding of the matrix.

8. Numerical experiments. The experiments we perform to validate our quan-tum algorithm are aimed in three different directions: 1. correctness of the circuit and of its QCLAB [16] implementation; 2. tightness of the error bound; 3. comparison with FABLE [6] in terms of circuit depth and size. We run our tests on three different classes of generator-representable matrices. The first class, Random , is obtained by constructing the semiseparable matrix S(u, v )

where u and v are normalized random vectors with entries drawn independently from the standard normal distribution (zero mean, unit variance). The second family, which we call Tridiagonal , consists of generators extracted from the semiseparable inverse of a random symmetric tridiagonal matrix, constrained to be irreducible and diagonally dominant. The generators are obtained in a numerically stable way from the first and last columns of the inverse, which are proportional to v and u respec-tively, each obtained by solving a single tridiagonal linear system; the semiseparable matrix is then reconstructed from the normalized generators to enforce the unit-norm assumption. The third class, denoted Exponential , is constructed as follows: for 

i = 0, . . . , N − 1 we set ui = eθt i and vi = e−θt i , where ti = i/N and, in our tests, θ = 3. Once normalized, these generators have two useful features: they are the generators of the covariance matrix of a stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (Gauss–Markov) process sampled on a uniform grid, and, being samples of a smooth exponential, they can be prepared as quantum states by a circuit of polylogarithmic depth [18]. In this particular case, since eθi /N = ∏ k(eθ2k /N )bk

factorizes over the binary digits bk of i,the normalized state is a product state that can be prepared exactly by n = log 2 N

single-qubit Ry rotations, with no entangling gates. The code reproducing all experiments is available both in QCLAB [16] and Qiskit [15] at https://github.com/Giacomo-Antonioli/Semiseparable Block Encoding.

9. Conclusions. We have presented an explicit quantum circuit that block-encodes a generator-representable semiseparable matrix directly from its two genera-tors, without forming any factorization or using complicated hierarchical decomposi-26 G. ANTONIOLI, G. M. DEL CORSO, R. VAN BEEUMEN 

tions. For an N × N one-pair matrix with N = 2n, the construction uses 2 n + 2 ancilla qubits, has O(polylog N ) depth, and realizes a (2√N , 2n + 2, 2√2 ε)-block encoding, where ε is the accuracy of the state preparation that loads the generators. The same construction extends to the unsymmetric one-pair class, at the cost of a controlled state preparation and of replacing the Hadamard gates on the triangular qubit by a suitable Ry rotation. In general, our results illustrate that the data-sparsity of a matrix can be turned into a concrete quantum advantage, mirroring the role that structure plays in classical fast algorithms. Several extensions appear within reach of the techniques developed here. The ex-tension to r-semiseparable matrices, where the rank of upper and lower part is at most 

r, to generator representable plus diagonal, a subclass of quasiseparable matrices [11], is straightforward combining our construction through a two-term linear combination of unitaries. However, the extension to non-generator-representable structure is still an open problem. The one-pair case is the flat, single-level instance of the broader family of rank-structured matrices. It would be interesting to explore whether the idea behind our algorithm can be applied recursively across the levels of an HODLR or HSS partition, so as to build a block encoding of a hierarchically semiseparable matrix without resorting to a level-by-level linear combination of unitaries [23, 26].
