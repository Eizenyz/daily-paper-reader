Abstract 

Analogue and digital Hamiltonian simulation represent two distinct approaches to making one quantum system replicate the physical prop-erties of another, with consequences ranging from practical applications to complexity theory and quantum gravity. They are rooted in two dis-tinct regimes of quantum dynamics: time-independent Hamiltonians for the former; fully controllable time-dependent quantum circuits in the lat-ter. Time-periodic Hamiltonians represent an intermediate regime. In this work, we establish a theory of Floquet simulation where we use periodically driven Hamiltonians to synthesise time-independent ones. We show that a set of interactions S can Floquet-simulate every Hamil-tonian in the Lie algebra Lie( S), and consequently we give a complete and constructive characterisation of Floquet-universal Hamiltonians that are able to produce any target Hamiltonian. Notably, our construction uses only O(1) local interaction strengths and ratios thereof, avoiding the impractical multi-scale local interaction strengths often required in analogue simulation via time-independent Hamiltonians. Instead, the con-struction requires a range of driving frequencies which scales polynomially with system size for important classes such as k-local lattice Hamiltonians. This has immediate complexity-theoretic implications, including numer-ous BQP-completeness and QMA-hardness results for natural physical properties and quantities studied in Floquet physics.

# Overview 

# 1 Introduction 

Quantum simulation is both of immense practical value and fundamental scien-tific significance. On the practical front, simulating the quantum many-body systems that occur in chemistry and materials science is one of the most promis-ing applications of quantum technology [Dal+22]. Analogue simulation, in which a quantum system is engineered to reproduce a target Hamiltonian of inter-est, has been successfully performed for decades, at scales well-beyond what is possible on classical or quantum computers [BL20; Mon+21]. Meanwhile, recent results have reported the first scientifically meaningful digital quantum simulations that challenge current state-of-the-art classical techniques [Ala+26; Ala+25]. Fundamentally, quantum simulation has deep connections to quantum computation and complexity theory. It was established long ago that general-purpose digital quantum simulation is theoretically equivalent to a universal quantum computer [Llo96; Dod+02]. Meanwhile, analogue quantum simulation is closely related to Hamiltonian complexity theory [CMP18; CM16; BH17], with recent theoretical results proving an exact equivalence between general-purpose analogue quantum simulators and Hamiltonians in the QMA complexity class [Koh+22]. These qualitatively different sets of simulation experiments and theorems, analogue and digital, represent two distinct mathematical structures underlying the simulation. In analogue simulation, a static, time-independent Hamiltonian is engineered to replicate the desired target Hamiltonian. This form of simulation was originally proposed in [Jak+98], and a full mathematical theory underlying it worked out in [CMP18]. Meanwhile, in digital quantum simulation, a quantum circuit consisting of a sequence of few-qubit unitary gates is constructed, which reproduces the time-dynamics of the target Hamiltonian. Its theory is exactly that of universal quantum computation and the Hamiltonian simulation algorithm, worked out in the 1980-90s [NC10]. Thus analogue quantum simulation (at least in its original form) uses a time-independent Hamiltonian with no dynamically varying parameters to carry out the simulation. Whereas digital quantum simulation uses a quantum com-puter, which can be viewed as a highly time-dependent Hamiltonian with many dynamically varying parameters. The consequences of this underlying mathematical difference can be traced all the way through from theory to state-of-the-art experiments. On the theoretical side, it is a classic result from the early days of quantum computing theory that any entangling two-qubit interaction (together with single-qubit gates) is sufficient to carry out universal quantum computation [Dod+02], hence sufficient for digital simulation of any desired target Hamiltonian. Whereas the classification of two-qubit interactions sufficient for universal analogue simulation is non-trivial, does not encompass all entangling interactions, and was worked out only 3in 2018 [CMP18]. Experimentally, because analogue simulation only requires engineering time-independent Hamiltonians, it is less demanding of hardware, hence far larger analogue quantum simulation experiments have been performed than digital. On the other hand, since only time-independent Hamiltonians are available, the range of target Hamiltonians that can feasibly be simulated using analogue techniques is more restricted. There is an interesting intermediate regime between time-independent Hamil-tonians with no dynamical control, and time-dependent Hamiltonians with full dynamical control: periodically driven Hamiltonians, also called “Floquet Hamiltonians”. Their time-dependence is periodic:

As with analogue quantum simulation, Floquet quantum systems have been engineered and studied experimentally for multiple decades. They exhibit in-teresting many-body physics phenomena, many of which parallel those of static many-body quantum systems [MP15; Khe+16], but some of which are not possi-ble with time-independent Hamiltonians, most famously time-crystals [EBN16; MIQ+22]. Simulation of Floquet systems using digital quantum computers has become popular recently [MIQ+22; Zha+22], largely because the circuit depths attainable in current quantum computers are typically insufficient to achieve a good approximation to continuous-time dynamics. Although these are discrete Floquet systems consisting of repeated layers of quantum gates, rather than the sinusoidally periodically-driven Hamiltonians of conventional Floquet physics. However, the use of Floquet Hamiltonians themselves to simulate other quantum Hamiltonians remains less explored than either the static or fully time-dependent cases. In this paper, we initiate the systematic, mathemati-cal study of quantum Floquet simulation: specifically, the use of periodically driven Hamiltonians to simulate target Hamiltonians that are time-independent. We prove a complete characterisation of the Floquet-simulation power of any set of interactions, and as a consequence fully characterise Floquet-universal Hamiltonians able to simulate any time-independent Hamiltonians. The classification is simple to state, although its proof is non-trivial: a set of periodically driven interactions S can Floquet-simulate any Hamiltonian in the Lie algebra Lie( S) generated by S. In particular, this means that a set of interactions can simulate all target Hamiltonians – is “Floquet universal” – if it generates the full Lie algebra. Comparing this characterisation with the time-independent and fully time-dependent cases, we see that the condition for Floquet-universality coincides 4with the classification of interactions that generate universal gates for quantum computation. Hence, restricting from discontinuous time-dependent Hamiltoni-ans with infinite frequency bandwidth to smoothly time-periodic Hamiltonians with bounded bandwidth does not reduce the computational or simulation power. This contrasts to restricting to time-independent control, which was shown in [CMP18; Koh+22] to in general reduce both. Despite arriving at the same classification, the proof in the Floquet case is very different and significantly more involved than the case of universal quantum gates. It uses a novel algebraic geometry argument via residues to relate the effective Hamiltonian to the canonical projection from the free associative algebra to the Lie algebra, and requires a recent tighter bound on the tail of the Magnus expansion [ACO25]. Our characterisation of the Floquet-simulation power of an arbitrary set of interactions is also constructive: given any target Hamiltonian, suitable periodic driving parameters for generating that Hamiltonian can be derived by essentially a linear algebra computation. Notably, all these results still hold when all ratios between local interaction strengths in the engineered Floquet Hamiltonian bounded between 1 and 2 (in natural units) so that only the driving frequencies are allowed to scale. This is a key practical motivation for this work, contrasting strongly with the time-independent case where the the ratios of the interaction strengths scale polynomially, and there are no-go results indicating that this cannot be im-proved [CMP18; AZ19; Har+24; AB25]. For this reason, analogue Hamiltonian simulation constructions based on perturbation gadgets or history state construc-tions have had little practical impact, as they require precise engineering of local interaction strengths across different energy scales, well beyond capabilities of analogue experiments. On the other hand, engineering finite bandwidth periodic driving is potentially more experimental feasible – indeed, this type of driving has already been realised experimentally [Jot+14; Cho+20; Koy+25]. Since universality encompasses the simulation of arbitrary Hamiltonians, in-cluding families with exponentially many independent parameters and QMA hard instances, unsurprisingly the driving frequencies in the general Floquet-universal construction can grow exponentially with the size of the target Hamiltonian. The relevant question is whether an efficient construction exists for Hamiltoni-ans with a succinct description. We show that it does for the important class of k-local Hamiltonians on a lattice, and more generally k-local Hamiltonians whose non-commutativity graph has constant chromatic number. For any such Hamiltonian on n qudits, Floquet simulation can be achieved with all interaction strengths O(1) and all driving frequencies bounded by poly( n). In the next section we will review Floquet theory before defining Floquet simulation and establishing its broader consequences. We then present our two main results in Section 4: the characterisation of the simulation power of Floquet Hamiltonians including a description of Floquet-universal Hamiltonians; and

E.g. approximating the minimum quasienergies to 1 /poly pre-cision is QMA-hard, approximating the quasienergy spectrum is BQP-complete in the same setting in which spectral approximation for static Hamiltonians is, etc. It is worth highlighting here the differences between our Floquet simulation results and classic Trotterization-based quantum algorithms for Hamiltonian simulation. That family of quantum algorithms simulates the time dynamics of a quantum Hamiltonian by constructing a sequence of quantum gates that approximates time-evolution under the target Hamiltonian for a small time-step, and then repeats this series of gates. If we represent the quantum circuit for such a Trotter algorithm as a time-dependent Hamiltonian, then this Hamiltonian is indeed time-periodic, as the sequence of gates is repeated. Such circuits are sometimes called “Floquet circuits”. But the time dependence in this Hamiltonian is very far from a finite sum of smooth sinusoids; it requires “bang-bang” control, i.e. the local Hamiltonians must be switched on and off discontinuously, equivalent to square-wave driving whose Fourier series has infinite bandwidth.

8Notably, while it is significant that in the above construction all the amplitudes are O(1) and their ratios are between 1 and 2, in the general case the frequencies have unfavourable scaling with the size of the target Hamiltonian being simulated. Our second result shows that for an important class of target Hamiltonian, including k-local lattice Hamiltonians, this Floquet-simulation can be performed efficiently. In this setting, in addition to all the interaction strengths and their ratios being O(1), the driving frequencies scale only polynomially in the number of parameters in the target Hamiltonian. 

Theorem 12 (Floquet efficiency) k-local Hamiltonians whose interaction graph has constant chromatic number (which includes all lattice Hamiltoni-ans) can be simulated by Floquet-universal Hamiltonians with all amplitude ratios bounded between 1 and 2, as above, and with all frequencies in the Fourier series scaling polynomially with the size of the Hamiltonian being simulated. 

This result is shown by careful accounting of the requirements on the driving frequencies in Section 10.4. A formal statement of the efficiency result is given in Theorem 57. 

# 5 Proof roadmap 

The proof of Theorem 11 is somewhat lengthy and is organised into four key steps. Each step is captured by a separate theorem and developed in its own section of Part II. In Section 7, we prove that given certain conditions on the driving frequencies in the periodic Hamiltonian the lower order terms of the Magnus expansion vanish (Theorem 19). The remaining leading order term consists of a linear combination of nested commutators of terms appearing in the time-periodic Hamiltonian. This term divides into a product of two distinct parts: an outer sum over permutations of nested commutators of elements, and an inner sum over permutations of combinatorial functions of the driving frequencies (Theorem 21). Section 8 deals with the second of these two parts. We essentially show that the combinatorial functions appearing in the sum form a complete basis of rational functions in a suitable space. The driving frequencies can therefore always be chosen such that the inner sum effectively reduces to the identity, leaving just the outer sum over nested commutators of generators. Ironically, showing that this combinatorial factor can be simplified away entirely is the most involved part of the proof, culminating in Theorem 37. Section 9 then shows in Theorem 40 that the remaining outer sum is in fact the canonical projection of words in the free associative algebra over the generators into the Lie algebra over the same. This implies that any Lie polynomial of fixed degree generated by the terms in the original Hamiltonian can be realised in the leading order term in the Magnus expansion. The final step in Section 10 addresses the challenge of combining Lie-polynomial terms appearing at different orders in the Magnus expansion, while controlling the unwanted cross-terms and the infinite tail of the expansion. The solution is two-fold. First, we show in Lemma 44 that rescaling of the relative 9frequencies alone allows us to tune the effective magnitudes of terms in the Magnus expansion, allowing terms of different orders to be combined successfully. Secondly, recent new error bounds on the Magnus expansion due to some of the authors [ACO25] give sufficiently tight control on the norm of the tail. This leads to the formal statement of our main Floquet universality result Theorem 49. To analyse the scaling behaviour of the frequency coefficients for the simu-lation of an arbitrary target Hamiltonian, Lemma 50 first provides a bound in terms of the number of polynomials and their degree. We then restrict the setting in Section 10.4 by assuming a commutation structure of the target decomposition; in Lemma 55, commutation between the generators of distinct terms allows the same frequency vectors to be reused within each family, while constant phase shifts bring contributions of different degrees to a common scale. Consequently, the frequency bound depends on the number of families and the maximal Lie polynomial degree, but is independent of the total number of terms within each family. For local Hamiltonians, Lemma 56 provides the required decomposition by colouring the intersection graph, so that the number of commuting families is controlled by locality and the chromatic number. In particular, lattice Hamiltoni-ans admit such a decomposition with a constant number of families. Combining this with Lemma 55 and the bounded amplitude construction above yields The-orem 12: k-local lattice Hamiltonians, and more generally those with constant chromatic number, can be Floquet-simulated with polynomially bounded driving frequencies while all physical amplitudes remain O(1) and within a ratio of 2.

23 8.2 Shuffle identities and image of the functional Ξ

We now turn from the local pole structure of individual denominators to the global linear relations among the family {Ξ( π, ·)}π∈SK . The residue machinery above will later be used to prove that a fixed-last-letter subfamily is linearly independent. These are governed by shuffle identities: interleaving two ordered subwords gives a partial-fraction identity, which vanishes on the zero-sum hyperplane H0.

Definition 28 (Shuffle product) Let α and β be two disjoint, non-empty ordered words whose union contains exactly the letters of

This new admissibility condition prevents unintended interactions between frequency sets corresponding to different Lie polynomials, as we will show shortly. Nonetheless, even with this condition, intermediate orders in the Magnus expansion can still give rise to unwanted mixed terms involving different frequency sets. These mixed terms contribute to the error and are accounted for by a general intermediate-order error bound. One challenge of combining Lie polynomials of different degrees is that their leading Magnus contributions carry different powers of the fundamental frequency. We will show that these degree-dependent scalings can be compensated for, and the correct relative weights of the target terms can be generated, via integer dilations of the driving frequencies alone, without scaling the amplitudes.

## 10.4 Efficiency of Floquet Simulation 

In the previous section, we arrived in Theorem 49 at a characterisation of the Floquet simulation power of sets of interactions and, as an immediate consequence, a complete characterisation of Floquet-universal Hamiltonians which can simulate any possible target Hamiltonian in this way. Moreover, this can be achieved whilst keeping all the interaction strengths O(1), with only the frequencies scaling as a function of the system size. Now, an arbitrary Hamiltonian on 

n qudits contains exponentially (in n) many independent parameters. Since Floquet-universality requires the ability to simulate any Hamiltonian whatsoever, a simple counting argument implies that at least some of the parameters in any Floquet simulation construction must scale exponentially in general. And we see this explicitly in eq. (259), where the frequencies depend exponentially on 

Kmax , the maximum degree in the Lie polynomial decomposition of the target Hamiltonian, which scales with n in general. However, for Hamiltonians that are specified by a polynomial (in n) number of parameters, we might hope for an efficient Floquet-simulation, where all parameters scale at most polynomially with the system size. In this section, we show exactly this for the most important class of efficiently-specifiable Hamiltonians: k-local Hamiltonians on a lattice: Floquet-simulation of any such Hamiltonian on n qudits can be performed with all amplitudes O(1) and all frequencies poly( n). In fact, we prove this for a slightly more general class of Hamiltonians: k-local Hamiltonian whose non-commutativity graph has 52 constant chromatic number, of which lattice Hamiltonians are an important special case. To show this, we need a slight strengthening of Lemma 50, in which frequency coefficients are reused within commuting families and constant phase shifts are introduced to keep the required integer dilations independent of the number of terms in each family.
