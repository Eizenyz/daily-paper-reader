Abstract 

Variational quantum algorithms have emerged as a promising approach for simulating open quantum systems, yet their expressive power and trainabil-ity remain poorly understood. We propose a variational framework for solving Lindblad master equations by minimizing a residual cost function using a com-positional ansatz obtained by combining a classical neural network with aparameterized quantum circuit. The density matrix is represented through a purification on an enlarged Hilbert space, ensuring positivity, Hermiticity, and unit trace by construction, while all gradients are evaluated exactly via the parameter-shift rule. Beyond the algorithmic formulation, we derive an upper bound for the covering number of the proposed compositional model and compare it with that of purely quantum neural networks. Our analysis shows that the clas-sical parametrization enlarges the effective hypothesis class while providing finer control over its complexity, thereby establishing a theoretical connection between expressivity, covering numbers, effective dimension, and the barren plateau phe-nomenon. Numerical experiments validate both the accuracy of the proposed 

1arXiv:2610.22691v1 [quant-ph] 15 Oct 2026 method for representative Lindblad master equations and the theoretical predic-tions regarding gradient concentration. These results provide a unified variational and theoretical framework for the simulation of dissipative quantum dynamics using compositional quantum machine learning models.  

> Keywords: Lindblad master equations; Open quantum systems; Quantum neural networks; Variational quantum simulation; Covering numbers; Barren plateaus

# 1 Introduction 

The interaction of a quantum system with its environment generally gives rise to nonunitary reduced dynamics, which may manifest through dissipation, relaxation, or decoherence. Under the standard assumptions concerning the initial preparation of the system and its environment, this evolution can be described in terms of quantum channels, namely, completely positive trace-preserving linear maps [1, 2]. Such chan-nels admit a Kraus-operator representation and, equivalently, a unitary dilation on an enlarged Hilbert space [3, 4]. The form of the reduced dynamics is governed by the timescale over which the environment retains information about the system. When environmental correlations decay on a timescale much shorter than that characterizing the system dynamics, memory effects may be neglected and a Markovian approximation becomes appropri-ate. If these correlations persist, the evolution also depends on the system’s history and may be formulated in terms of integro-differential equations with memory kernels, derived by means of projection-operator techniques [5, 6]. In the time-homogeneous Markovian regime, the evolution can be described by a completely positive trace-preserving dynamical semigroup whose generator takes the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad form [7, 8]. The corresponding equation, commonly referred to as the Lindblad master equation or the GKSL equation, combines a coherent contribution generated by the Hamiltonian with a dissipator describing processes induced by the system–environment interaction. The superoper-ator comprising both contributions is termed the Lindblad generator, or Lindbladian, whereas the GKSL equation provides the standard framework for describing Marko-vian open quantum systems [1, 9, 10]. These dynamics are central to quantum metrology, energy transport in molecular systems, and dissipative state preparation for applications in quantum computation and quantum information [11–13]. Despite their broad relevance, the numerical treatment of the GKSL master equation rapidly becomes computationally demanding as the system size increases, owing to the exponential growth of both state and operator spaces [2]. This dif-ficulty has motivated the development of compact representations and variational methods that avoid a complete parametrization of the density matrix. Within the quantum-circuit model, an additional structural mismatch arises between the unitary transformations implemented by the circuit and the generally nonunitary evolution of 2the reduced system. A dilation on an enlarged Hilbert space provides a unitary real-ization of quantum channels through the introduction of ancillary degrees of freedom followed by a partial trace [3, 4, 14]. The simulation of such dynamics has prompted strategies based on product for-mulas for local Liouvillians [15], linear-combination techniques adapted to quantum channels and completely positive maps [16], unitary decompositions of nonunitary operators [17], and variational formulations for mixed states and open-system pro-cesses [18, 19]. The choice of ansatz, measurement overhead, numerical stability, and allocation of classical and quantum resources remain central considerations in these methods [20]. Recent advances in quantum machine learning have opened new possibilities for representing and simulating quantum systems through parameterized models. Fol-lowing the success of neural-network quantum states for many-body problems [21], parameterized quantum circuits have emerged as expressive function approxima-tors compatible with noisy intermediate-scale quantum hardware. Quantum–classical algorithms, in which a parameterized quantum circuit is optimized through classi-cal routines, have become one of the dominant paradigms in variational quantum computing due to their flexibility and hardware efficiency [22–24]. These developments have naturally been extended to open quantum systems. Clas-sical neural-network (CNN) representations have been proposed for approximating dissipative many-body dynamics [25] and nonequilibrium steady states [26], while deep quantum neural network (QNN)s have recently been employed to solve Lindblad mas-ter equations variationally [27]. More generally, differentiable quantum circuits have demonstrated their ability to approximate the solutions of differential equations by minimizing suitably designed residual functions rather than performing direct time integration [28]. Such residual-based formulations transform the solution of the gov-erning equation into an optimization problem and have become increasingly attractive within variational quantum algorithms. The effectiveness of these methods, however, depends critically on the expressive power and trainability of the underlying variational ansatz. On the one hand, increas-ing the complexity of a quantum circuit generally enlarges the family of states that can be represented. On the other hand, highly expressive parameterized quantum cir-cuits may suffer from barren plateaus, where the variance of the gradients decreases exponentially with the number of qubits [29]. Subsequent studies have shown that this phenomenon strongly depends on the choice of cost function [30] and may also be induced by highly entangled variational states [31]. Consequently, understanding the relationship between expressivity and trainability has become one of the central challenges in quantum machine learning. Several theoretical frameworks have recently been developed to characterize the expressive capabilities of quantum neural networks. For example, dynamical Lie alge-bras have recently been employed to analyse the expressive power and generalization properties of variational quantum models [32]. From the perspective of statistical learning theory, concepts such as effective dimension and covering numbers provide quantitative measures of model complexity and approximation capability [33, 34]. In particular, covering numbers quantify the minimum number of metric balls required to 3cover a hypothesis class and play a central role in approximation theory and learning theory [35–37]. Besides their importance in generalization analysis, covering numbers also provide insight into concentration phenomena in high-dimensional spaces [38], thereby establishing a theoretical link with the emergence of barren plateaus. Motivated by these considerations, in this work we propose a variational frame-work for solving Lindblad master equations by minimizing a residual cost function constructed from physically relevant observables. The density matrix is represented through a purification ansatz on an enlarged Hilbert space, thereby guaranteeing posi-tivity, Hermiticity, and unit trace by construction. Rather than optimizing the rotation angles of the quantum circuit directly, we define them through a classical neural net-work, so that the resulting variational ansatz is obtained by composing a classical neural network with a parameterized quantum circuit. In this way, the classical model generates a smooth parametrization of the quantum circuit, while the latter maps these parameters into a purified quantum state. This compositional construction combines the expressive capabilities of quantum neural networks with the flexibility of classi-cal function approximation. Furthermore, all gradients of the proposed residual cost function can be evaluated exactly by means of the parameter-shift rule [39], making the method compatible with current quantum hardware. Beyond the algorithmic formulation, we investigate how this compositional archi-tecture influences both the expressive power and the trainability of the resulting variational model. To this end, we derive an upper bound for its covering number and compare it with the corresponding bound for a purely quantum neural network. Our analysis shows that composing a parameterized quantum circuit with a classical neu-ral network enlarges the effective hypothesis class while providing finer control over its complexity through the classical parametrization. Building upon these results, we establish a connection between covering numbers, the effective dimension of the varia-tional manifold, and the barren plateau phenomenon, thereby providing a theoretical explanation of the trainability properties of the proposed model. Finally, numerical experiments illustrate both the capability of the proposed approach to solve represen-tative Lindblad master equations and the theoretical predictions concerning gradient concentration and optimization behaviour.

# 3 Expressivity and plateau barren problem 

Expressivity refers to a model’s ability to represent a wide range of functions, determining the smallest achievable training error. We characterize the expressiveness of the composition of a QNN and a CNN using the covering number, an advanced tool from statistical learning theory. In mathematics, the covering number N (ε, HS )is defined as the minimum number of balls of radius ε > 0 required to cover a space 9HS , allowing overlaps between balls [33, 34]. The covering number quantifies the size of a set and applies to general metric spaces. Consequently, the number of functions that can be represented explodes as the number of qubits increases, but grows sublinearly (and with greater controllability) as the number of classical parameters increases. These observations show that fine-tuning of our model should be carried out pri-marily by increasing the number of classical parameters nw, while the number of qubits serves to expand the landscape exponentially. Thus, the following practical recipe should be followed when training our model: 

• First, fix a small number of qubits and increase the number of classical parameters 

nw of the neural network until the desired behaviour is captured.

# 5 Conclusions 

In this work, we have developed a variational framework for minimizing Lind-blad’s residual using an ansatz based on quantum neural networks (QNNs) whose rotation parameters are generated by a classical neural network (CNN). A central contribution of this study is the demonstration that the gradients of the Lindblad residual cost function can be computed exactly and efficiently via the parameter-shift rule, enabling the use of quantum hardware for gradient evaluation. Beyond the optimization aspects, we have analysed the expressivity of the pro-posed hybrid architecture through its covering number. By comparing the upper bound obtained for our model with the covering number of pure QNNs, we have shown that 23 the effective dimension of the reachable state manifold expands by a factor propor-tional to the number of classical parameters nw. This reveals that the classical network acts as a controllable mechanism that smoothly deforms the quantum parameter space, allowing the model to represent a richer family of functions without requiring an expo-nential increase in the number of qubits. In particular, the degrees of freedom of the model grow linearly with nw, whereas in pure QNNs they grow exponentially with the number of qubits. This leads to a practical training strategy: first increase nw

until the desired behaviour is captured, and only then increase the number of qubits if necessary. The covering number analysis also provides a theoretical bridge to the barren plateau phenomenon. For pure QNNs, the variance of the gradient decays exponen-tially with the number of qubits, as predicted by the concentration properties of high-dimensional manifolds. In contrast, the hybrid QNN–CNN architecture allows modulating the effective dimension through nw, thereby preventing the complete van-ishing of gradients even when the number of qubits is kept low. This theoretical insight is fully consistent with the numerical experiments: global observables lead to exponen-tial decay of the gradient variance, while the Lindblad residual cost function—built from local observables—mitigates barren plateaus both in pure and hybrid settings. Overall, the study shows that hybrid QNN–CNN architectures provide a flexible, expressive, and scalable approach for minimizing Lindblad’s residual and approximat-ing dissipative quantum dynamics. The ability to compute exact gradients via the parameter-shift rule, together with the controllability offered by the classical param-eters, results in favourable optimization landscapes and improved expressivity. These findings support the use of hybrid variational models as a promising tool for quantum simulation on noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices.
