# Cayley access models for matrix functions: reachable class and cost 

Gianna M. Del Corso 

> Department of Computer Science, University of Pisa, Italy

We study how to compute f (A)b on a quantum computer, where A is Hermi-tian. When f is nonsmooth or has localized difficulty in the spectrum, classical numerical linear algebra approximates it with rational functions rather than polynomials. We propose the Cayley transform, parameterized by σ ∈ R and γ > 0, as an alternative to the standard quantum access model. The two parameters let one concentrate accuracy where f is difficult, which standard models can achieve only by raising the polynomial degree globally. This transformation converts Hermitian matrices to unitaries, allowing generalized quantum signal processing to apply Laurent polynomials bounded on the unit circle. We compare three access models (block encoding, matrix exponential, and Cayley encoding) in terms of query complexity and of the cost of their circuit realization. When the difficulty of f is localized in the spectrum, the Cayley encoding reduces the required degree by a factor of three to four, and it repre-sents the shifted resolvent exactly at degree one. For spectral projectors, the standard models cannot reach the target accuracy at fixed degree, because their approximants cannot concentrate resolution near the discontinuity. Realizing the Cayley encoding from a block encoding requires solving a shifted linear system, whose cost offsets its query-complexity gain over the standard models. For structured matrices arising in practice, however, low-depth circuits for the encoding can be constructed directly, and the gain is preserved at the circuit level.

For matrices of moderate size several methods are available [1], but when N is very large, forming or diagonalizing A is out of reach and one looks for algorithms whose cost is polynomial in log N . This is the setting of quantum linear algebra, and the one considered in this paper. Computing f (A)b on a quantum computer poses two distinct subproblems. • Approximating f . A quantum computer applies unitary operations and reaches A

f : it applies to the eigenvalues of A an approximant of f rather than f itself, as done in classical computation. That approximant is usually a polynomial, and its degree controls the cost. When f is nonsmooth, or has a discontinuity or a pole near the spectrum, polynomials converge slowly and the degree becomes prohibitive. Classical numerical linear algebra turns to rational approximation in these cases, and we ask here whether the same can be done on a quantum computer. • Encoding A. A quantum algorithm cannot see A directly; it accesses it through an access model, that is a unitary circuit, from which A can be inferred. The reachable functions are dictated jointly by the access model and by the transformation applied to it. Two access models are standard, block encoding [2] and matrix-exponential (also known as Hamiltonian simulation) [3]. In this paper we investigate the Cayley transform as an alternative access model for a Hermitian A when the goal is to compute f (A)b. It is the matrix version of the Möbius map zσ,γ (λ) = ( λ − σ − iγ )/(λ − σ + iγ ) sending the real line to the unit circle, and applied to A it produces a unitary matrix. The logic of the construction is the following; GQSP [4] already applies to a unitary any Laurent polynomial of it bounded by one on the unit circle, with one ancilla qubit. Taking Cσ,γ (A) as that unitary, whose eigenvalues are Möbius images of the eigenvalues of A, the Laurent polynomial becomes, back in λ, a rational function of A.We concentrate our study on the following aspects. We show that the reachable func-tions are the rational functions of type (2 d, 2d) whose poles lie at σ ± iγ , each of order at most d. Unlike polynomials, this class represents exactly functions with poles at σ ±iγ , but it does not coincide with the rational functions of the same degree with freely chosen poles. Multiplying Cayley factors extends it to arbitrary pole configurations, and we show that the resulting family is the only one available, since the finite products of Cayley factors are the rational functions unitary on every Hermitian matrix. Our model has tunable parameters that adapt the encoding to the problem at hand. In the standard access models the unitary is fixed by A alone, the normalization α and the evolution time t depending only on the matrix, and only the polynomial is constructed on f . Here σ and γ decide where in the spectrum the approximation is accurate and how finely, so choosing them already records where f is difficult. As in the matrix-exponential model, one ancilla suffices and no subnormalization constant appears. The cost of the construction depends on how the encoding is realized. In the query model, where Cσ,γ (A) is supplied as an oracle, the degree needed is most of the time far lower than for the two standard models. Building that oracle from a block encoding of A

requires solving a shifted linear system, whose cost has to be weighed against the saving. On a generic matrix, this cost per query cancels the reduction in the number of queries, so the total cost is of the same order as in the standard models. The advantage therefore survives only for matrices whose structure allows the Cayley encoding to be built directly as a low-depth circuit. 

21.1 Contributions 

This paper proposes the Cayley transform as a rational access model for matrix functions on a quantum computer, and examines what it can compute, what it costs, and when it is worth using. At the level of expressive power, we show that processing Cσ,γ (A) by GQSP with a Laurent polynomial of degree d reaches the rational functions of type (2 d, 2d) whose poles lie at σ ± iγ , each of order at most d, with one ancilla and no parity restriction (Theorem 3.1). Approximating f then reduces to trigonometric approximation on the circle (Proposition 3.4), so the classical theory of polynomial approximation carries over to a rational problem through a change of variable. The parameters σ and γ enter the encoding itself, concentrating the accuracy where f is difficult before any polynomial is chosen, which the fixed access models cannot do. The three access models become comparable through the accuracy density, the derivative of the map each of them induces on the spectrum, which measures how much phase a model assigns to a region of the spectrum and therefore how finely a given degree can distinguish eigenvalues there. Products of Cayley factors place the poles at any finite set of points off the real axis, still with a single ancilla. We show that these are exactly the rational functions unitary on every Hermitian matrix (Theorem 4.2), so no other rational unitary encoding exists, and that on the targets considered here the extra poles bring no advantage. On the side of cost we separate the query model from the realization model. In the query model, where the encoding is supplied as an oracle, the number of applications is smaller than for the two standard models on the functions that polynomials approximate slowly. Whether this carries over to circuit cost depends on how the encoding is built. In Section 6 we realize Cσ,γ (A) from a block encoding of A, and we show that the reduction in degree that a narrow γ buys is spent, at the same rate, in the shifted solve that each application then requires: the two parameters do not by themselves lower the cost. What the analysis leaves is a condition on A, namely that the encoding admit a circuit whose cost does not grow with 1/γ , and in Section 6.2 we identify classes of matrices of practical interest that meet it. Finally, we validate the analysis numerically (Section 7) by computing the best approximation in each reachable class, with the free parameter of each model optimized at every degree. The comparison is one of approximating power and of query counts; no circuit is simulated and no gate count is reported. At accuracy 10 −6 the Cayley class is the cheapest on six out of eight functions. The matrix exponential usually lies between the other two. It, too, is applied by processing a unitary, namely e−iAt , so the fact that the Cayley encoding needs fewer queries cannot be attributed to unitarity by itself: the gain instead comes from placing poles near a difficulty of f , which rational functions approximate better than polynomials can. On the family of Sturm–Liouville operators of Section 7.3 we measure the quantities that govern the structured realization. For functions whose variation is concentrated near the smallest eigenvalue, the Cayley class needs a degree independent of N , while the polynomial and the exponential approaches need a degree of order κ; with the encoding realized from the generators of the shifted resolvent, this gives a cost of ˜O(κ1/4) against ˜O(κ).

1.2 Relation to prior work 

Classically, there are many tools one can use to compute f (A)b: shift-and-invert and rational Krylov methods [5], contour-integral eigensolvers [6], Zolotarev’s optimal rational approximants [7], complex-shifted preconditioners for indefinite systems [8], and the Crank– Nicolson propagator [9]. Which of them performs best depends on the spectrum of A and on the regularity of f , and rational approximations have become the standard tool when 

3polynomial ones converge slowly, as happens with singularities close to the spectrum or poorly separated eigenvalues. The quantum setting has followed a similar path. Early algorithms relied on quantum phase estimation, which gives coherent access to the spectral decomposition: the Harrow– Hassidim–Lloyd algorithm implements f (λ) = 1 /λ , and later work extended the idea to fractional powers, exponentials, Gibbs states [10] and linear differential equations [11]. These methods need high-precision phase estimation and long coherent evolutions, which motivated frameworks acting directly on the spectrum. Quantum signal processing [3] showed that polynomial transformations of the eigenvalues of a unitary can be implemented by sequences of single-qubit rotations, and the quantum singular value transformation [2, 12] extended this to the singular values of a block-encoded matrix, unifying Hamiltonian simulation, linear system solvers, matrix inversion and Gibbs-state preparation. Building a block encoding is itself costly, and for a sparse matrix it needs several ancillas and multiqubit controlled operations. This problem was solved by two later frameworks, which take as input the time evolution e−iA , produced directly by a Trotter formula, and use a single ancilla. The first is quantum eigenvalue transformation of unitary matrices [13], which needs no multiqubit control either: writing eiΦ for an eigenvalue of the unitary, the method reaches the functions F (cos(Φ /2)) with F an even real polynomial. The second is the generalized version of signal processing [4, 14], which requires controlled access to the unitary but allows general SU (2) rotations, and reaches every Laurent polynomial bounded by one on the circle, with complex coefficients and no parity restriction. All of these frameworks are polynomial, and a polynomial is the wrong tool when the best approximant of f is rational. Two lines of work have addressed this. Contour-integral algorithms start from Cauchy’s formula and apply a quadrature rule to the integral, so that f (A)b is obtained from a set of shifted linear systems (zkI − A)xk = b weighted by the quadrature coefficients [15]. Shen, Van Buggenhout, Camps, Klymko and Van Beeumen [16] instead implement r(A) = ∑ 

> k

ck(zkI − A)−1 directly, with poles chosen at will and each resolvent built from linear combinations of matrix exponentials, at the price of ⌈log 2 m⌉ ancillas and a subnormalization factor for the combination. Our starting point is different. Rather than combining several resolvents, the Cayley transform turns the computation of a matrix function into a unitary signal-processing problem, with one ancilla and no overhead from a linear combination. It allows arbitrary pole placement, through products of Cayley factors, but the numerator is not free, being tied to the poles by the unitarity of the encoding. The construction therefore lies between the polynomial signal-processing methods and the rational approximations with a free numerator, and this intermediate position is a structural necessity. By Theorem 4.2 the class of rational functions unitary on every Hermitian matrix coincides with the finite products of Cayley factors, so no general rational encoding can be unitary and a method with a free numerator necessarily leaves the single-ancilla setting. Quantum Krylov methods, on the other hand, are still polynomial: the subspace is generated from real-time evolutions [17, 18] and not from resolvents, so their classical rational counterpart has no quantum analogue yet. Tong, An, Wiebe and Lin [19] consider a normal A with an efficiently implementable diagonalizing unitary, and observe that a block encoding of A−1 can then be built directly, by writing the eigenvalue in a register and inverting it with classical arithmetic, at a cost independent of κ(A). They use it as a preconditioner for (A + B)x = b with ∥A∥ ≫ ∥ B∥,and to evaluate e−β(A+B) through a quadrature of Cauchy’s formula, that is again through a linear combination of resolvents. The block encoding of A is thereby never formed, which is also what happens in Section 6.2, under a different structural hypothesis: fast inversion 

4needs a circuit for the eigenvector matrix of A and a formula for its eigenvalues, whereas our construction needs the inverse of the shifted matrix to be one-pair semiseparable, as for every irreducible tridiagonal A, and its generators to be prepared by a low-depth circuit.

Choosing the parameters in practice. The parameters are set by f and not by the eigenvalues of A, which are usually unknown: σ goes where f needs accuracy and γ is the scale of the region where the closest eigenvalues must be assigned different values. For a window, or for eigenvalues near a point τ , one takes σ = τ and γ about the half-width. The choice is a starting point rather than an optimum, since the cost of a degree-d approximant of width γ also depends on γ through the cost of one application; Section 7 shows that the pair minimizing the effective cost is not the one of smallest degree, a wider γ being often preferable because it lowers the rescaling.

In the query model the Cayley class reaches a prescribed accuracy at a degree between three and four times smaller than the polynomial one, on the functions whose difficulty is localized in part of the spectrum. For 1/λ on [1 /κ, 1] ∆ is 1/κ , the modulus of the smallest eigenvalue: near that point the function halves over an interval of that length, while near λ = 1 it varies far more slowly. Distinguishing two eigenvalues a distance ∆ apart near σ requires a degree d ≳ γ/ (2∆) by (4) , so the width that concentrates the accuracy is also what raises the degree, while by Lemma 6.1 a small width is what makes one application expensive.

In the classical case the cost of a nearby pole is paid once, since the factorization of A − wI is computed and then reused at every application, so the reduction in degree is a net gain and shift-and-invert is amortized over the iterations. In the quantum setting, on the contrary, each application of the encoding is a fresh solve costing ˜O(αM /γ ),so the nearby pole reduces the degree and raises the cost of one application at the same rate. The question therefore becomes when the encoding can be realized more cheaply than the construction above allows. 17 6.2 Structured families 

Encoding the shifted resolvent directly requires two properties of the matrix: that it can be described by few parameters, and that those parameters can be loaded as quantum states in polylogarithmic depth. If A A is real symmetric, tridiagonal and irreducible, the first property always holds, and the second holds in cases of interest that we describe below. Note that M = A − σI + iγI is then is complex symmetric and tridiagonal as well, and by the nullity theorem [31] its inverse R = M −1 is semiseparable of the one-pair kind, that is, can be described by two complex generators u and v as R = triu( uv T ) + tril( vu T , −1) .Although R is dense, a block encoding UR of R can be built from its generators alone, by an explicit circuit of depth O(log 3 N ) + Dsp , where Dsp is the depth of the routines that prepare u and v as quantum states [32]. UA is never formed and the construction of Section 6 is bypassed; the affine step Cσ,γ (A) = I − 2iγR and the amplification that follows do not depend on how UR was obtained. The second property, that the two generators can be prepared as quantum states in polylogarithmic depth, is not automatic, and is what selects the subclass on which this approach is effective. The encoding of [32] gives UR with subnormalization αR = 2 √N ∥u∥2∥v∥2, so the affine step Cσ,γ (A) = I − 2iγR has subnormalization β = 1 + 2 γα R = 1 + 4 γ√N ∥u∥2∥v∥2,and O(β) rounds of amplitude amplification recover the unitary. One application of the encoding therefore has depth O(β(log 3 N + Dsp )) , independent of κ(M ), against O(log 2 N )

for one query to UA when A is tridiagonal [33]. When the generators are prepared at polylogarithmic depth, as discussed below, one application of the Cayley encoding is thus more expensive than one query to UA. The cost of computing f (A)b, however, is the cost of one application multiplied by the number of applications, which is fixed by the degree of the approximant and by the rescaling η. 18 6.3 The reverse direction: structured unitaries 

One can also proceed in the opposite direction: start from a unitary U that is cheap to realize as a circuit and ask which Hermitian A has it as its Cayley transform. A natural candidate is the class of CMV matrices, the pentadiagonal unitaries obtained as the product of two layers of 2 × 2 unitary blocks on consecutive, offset index pairs, parametrized by Schur coefficients αk ∈ C, |αk| < 1 [34]. CMV matrices are a particular instance of the quasiseparable matrices of order one [35], those for which rank M (k : N, 1 : k − 1) = rank M (1 : k − 1, k : N ) = 1 for every k, that is, whose submatrices lying strictly below and strictly above the diagonal have rank one, the diagonal being unconstrained. The class contains the semiseparable-plus-diagonal matrices, obtained by adding a diagonal to a matrix all of whose strictly triangular submatrices come from a single pair of generators.

# 8 Conclusions and open problems 

We have proposed the Cayley transform as an access model for computing rational functions of a Hermitian matrix on a quantum computer. Used in association with generalized quantum signal processing, it produces the rational functions of type (2 d, 2d) whose poles lie at σ ± iγ , each of order at most d, with a single ancilla, no parity restriction, and none of the subnormalization incurred when a rational function is implemented as a linear combination of resolvents. Its two parameters σ and γ make the encoding tailored to f ,

24 so that accuracy can be concentrated where it is needed. We proved moreover that the products of Cayley factors are exactly the rational functions unitary on every Hermitian matrix, so the choice is forced rather than merely convenient. Through the change of variable to the circle, the design of the approximant becomes an ordinary trigonometric approximation problem, for which the classical theory is available unchanged. On the side of cost the encoding admits a unitary realization for every Hermitian 

A. In the query model a fixed-pole rational function is reached exactly at degree one, where a polynomial method pays a degree that grows as the pole approaches the spectrum. Whether this is still an advantage in the realization model depends on how the encoding is built. We showed that the generic construction from a block encoding of A cancels the gain (Corollary 6.4), and that structure in A can bypass that construction, exhibiting one family for which the shifted solve is obtained from the generators of the resolvent [32]. The first is the cost of loading the data that describe A. For a tridiagonal A the inverse of the shifted matrix is always one-pair semiseparable, but loading its generators as quantum states at polylogarithmic depth requires a regularity that holds for the discretized Sturm–Liouville operators of Section 7.3, with analytic coefficient, and is not guaranteed in general. The CMV construction of Section 6.3 raises the same question for its coefficients, together with that of characterizing the matrices whose Cayley transform is a CMV matrix. Beyond the tridiagonal case, natural candidates for a structured realization are hierarchical matrices, whose inverses retain a low-rank structure and for which block encodings are available [43], and indefinite operators from partial differential equations, for which the imaginary part of the shift keeps the resolvent well conditioned. The second direction is reaching the full rational class. A product of Cayley factors realizes the functions P (b(λ)) , whose numerator is tied to the denominator by unitarity, while the full class with poles at {wj , ¯wj }, each of order at most d, is reached by a linear combination of m GQSP circuits, at the cost of ⌈log 2 m⌉ further ancillas. Whether the full class can be reached with fewer ancillas, keeping the number of queries proportional to the degree, is a question we leave to future work. Another direction is a gate-level analysis. Theorem 6.2 counts queries, and concrete gate counts for the structured realization of Section 6.2, against QSVT applied to UA

and against QET-U with a Trotterized exponential, would turn the asymptotic statements into quantities one can weigh. On structured families one may also process the block-encoded resolvent directly, which is normal but not Hermitian, through quantum eigenvalue processing [44]: this avoids the amplification that makes the encoding unitary, at the price of a polynomial acting on a smaller spectral region, and the balance between the two remains to be assessed.
