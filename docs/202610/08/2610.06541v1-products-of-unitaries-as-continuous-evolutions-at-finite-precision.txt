PRODUCTS OF UNITARIES AS CONTINUOUS EVOLUTIONS AT FINITE PRECISION 

MICHAEL JARRET 

To Farzaneh Sabbagh, who had known me for three months when I was badly injured in a car accident, saw me through a long recovery with more patience than I had any right to ask, and then married me. This paper would not exist without her. 

Abstract. I present a short approach to discretizing continuous unitary evolution. It gives a correspondence in both directions between continuous unitary evolutions and products of unitaries in finite dimensions, and I use it to reduce discrete adiabatic theorems to continuous ones. At fine steps, holding truncated samples of a regulated Hamiltonian on a suitable finite partition gives an approximating product. Conversely, rounding the clock identifies a product of T unit-step samples with a continuous evolution, up to two half-step phases. I bound its operator-norm distance from the original evolution by O(1 /T ) through cancellation within each time step. This assumes bounded first and second derivatives, bounded spectral width, and eigenvalue differences bounded away from nonzero multiples of 2 π, uniformly in T . For a unitary walk with these derivative bounds, a fixed common free spectral arc supplies a Hermitian logarithm path satisfying the comparison hypotheses. Continuous simulation guarantees then transfer to the phase-corrected product, and continuous adiabatic bounds to the product itself, with an explicit additive O(1 /T ) error and each adiabatic theorem’s hypotheses retained. For positive definite quantum linear systems of condition number 

κ, this comparison and continuous adiabatic theory give a rescaled qubitized walk with O(κ/δ ) steps for leakage δ, matching the scaling previously obtained through a discrete adiabatic theorem. These products need no discrete adiabatic theorem. 

1. Discretizing unitary evolution 

Simulating continuous-time evolution within the quantum circuit model typically requires applying a finite product of unitaries or, equivalently, generating unitaries from a piecewise-constant Hamiltonian. Thus, a continuous evolution generated by H(t) is approximated by a finite product whenever a piecewise-constant Hamiltonian family G(t) generates approximately the same evolution. Holding matrix entries at finite precision can already define G.I use this observation to carry theorems between continuous evolutions and products of unitaries. In one direction, truncating a Hamiltonian’s entries builds a product, and conversely, under the hypotheses below, rounding time compares unit-step samples with the path’s own continuous evolution. Adding the comparison error to a continuous adiabatic bound then gives a discrete result that retains the continuous theorem’s hypotheses. We begin with bit truncation because it makes the connection concrete. Fix an energy unit and truncate each entry’s real and imaginary parts toward zero at the kth binary place, obtaining H(k). When this is constant on finitely many time intervals, its induced product W has one exponential per interval, with generator and duration determined by the truncation (Definition 2.1), and we compare it with the evolution 

U (T ) generated by H.This gives a finite product when the real and imaginary parts of the entries are piecewise monotone, but even a smooth entry can cross one truncation level infinitely often. Holding truncated midpoint samples on a chosen partition instead approximates every regulated family, one with one-sided limits at every time (Definition 2.3). Finite total variation ℓ, measured in operator norm, lets us control variation within intervals by placing checkpoints at equally spaced variation levels (Definition 3.5 and Lemma 3.6). In these fine-step constructions, we choose precision and refine the partition for accuracy.

At finite precision, products of unitaries and continuous evolutions are the same object, whichever we start from. Holding the entries at finite precision turns a continuous evolution into a product within √2 σ2−kT of it (Lemmas 3.4 and 3.8). Holding the clock at finite precision turns a product of unit steps from a smooth path with separated samples back into that path’s own evolution, up to the half-step phases and O(1 /T )(Theorem 6.4). Theorems therefore transfer between the two settings, and a theorist can derive a discrete result from its continuous counterpart instead of proving it again. The quantum linear systems problem shows what this buys, since there the continuous theorem and the comparison together recover the O(κ/δ )count that was previously obtained through a discrete adiabatic theorem (Corollaries A.2 and 6.9). Because the comparisons concern evolutions rather than adiabatic transport, I expect them to help wher-ever a time-dependent evolution is simulated. Existing methods choose their discretization from the Hamil-tonian in different ways. Wiebe, Berry, Høyer, and Sanders choose step lengths from the local size of the derivatives of H [29]. Berry, Childs, Su, Wang, and Wiebe rescale time by ∥H(t)∥, so that costs scale with R ∥H(t)∥ dt rather than T max t ∥H(t)∥ [4]. In Low and Wiebe’s interaction-picture algorithm, the average of ∥ ˙H∥ controls the number of discretization points and enters the cost logarithmically [22]. Poulin, Qarry, Somma, and Verstraete avoid smoothness assumptions altogether by sampling at random times [25]. Holding entries at finite precision needs neither derivatives nor random times, so I expect its equal-variation check-points to complement these methods. Each factor it produces is time-independent, and qubitization [21] can implement it. On analog hardware, a device whose finite-resolution controls truncate the entries of H in a fixed basis implements H(k)(t), so Lemma 3.4 applies to it, and so does Theorem 3.11 whenever H(k) is piecewise constant, as it is for piecewise-monotone H (Proposition 4.2). The correspondence is first order, and that leaves its main open questions, since some continuous theorems give much more than O(1 /T ). For gapped Gevrey families whose derivatives all vanish at the two ends, the continuous leakage has bounds of the form exp( −cT a), with c > 0 and 0 < a < 1 [13, 14]. Analytic Hamiltonians even have exponentially small estimates under suitable conditions at the two ends [17, 24, 15]. At fine steps, transferring such a bound means taking the evolution error ε comparable to it, and the precision 

k of Lemma 3.8, which is O(log( σT /ε )), then grows at most linearly in T . The factor counts of Lemma 3.8(2) and Section 4, however, grow like 2 k, which, for a fixed piecewise-monotone path rescaled to [0 , T ], is like T /ε 

> 18

and outgrows every power of T for these bounds. Can polynomially many factors retain the same leakage decay? At unit steps, the correction 1 − iY of Lemma 6.2 cancels only the linear drift, so the question becomes which additional corrections or endpoint hypotheses would let faster-decaying bounds transfer. A second question concerns growth in T . The time-discretization error of a sampled adiabatic product need not grow with T [30, 19, 1, 23], and I would like the same for entry truncation. At fixed precision k,when is its contribution to adiabatic leakage bounded independently of T ? Theorem 6.4 rounds the clock but keeps exact samples, so it does not answer this. Derivative bounds on H alone need not answer it either, because truncating a linearly moving coordinate produces a sawtooth error that could resonate with the spectral gap. A third question is whether the unit-step comparison extends to contracting evolutions [3], which Cunningham and Roland obtain by applying unitaries at Poisson-distributed times and averaging over realizations [9]. The obstacle is that the correction 1 − iY of Lemma 6.2 need not be a contraction. Finally, some costs lie outside this paper. Direct truncation needs the times at which entries cross trun-cation levels, which an oracle for H(t) does not return. Lemma 3.8(2) avoids this by computing checkpoints from the variation ℓ(t), or from any computable nondecreasing φ with ∥H(t) − H(s)∥ ≤ φ(t) − φ(s) for s ≤ t.For adiabatic applications, preparing a state in the initial spectral subspace and testing for the final one are further tasks.
