We develop an end-to-end framework for estimating canonical thermal properties of particle-number-conserving second-quantized Hamiltonians using quantum simulations. The framework in-tegrates initial state preparation with mixed Dicke states, particle-number-preserving Trotterized simulation, and generalized quantum signal processing to estimate canonical partition functions and observables. Building on this canonical scheme, we introduce a grand-canonical reconstruction scheme in which the same quantum measurement data can be reused for arbitrary chemical po-tentials through classical reweighting, thereby avoiding repeated quantum circuit executions as the chemical potential is varied. We prove the existence of system-size-independent temperature regimes in which both the canonical and reconstructed grand-canonical schemes have lower asymptotic time complexity than classical Lanczos methods, up to polynomial factors, for physically relevant Hamil-tonians. We numerically validate our theoretical results for the two-dimensional Hubbard model in both weak- and intermediate-coupling regimes. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Particle-number conservation is a fundamental symmetry of various quantum many-body Hamil-tonians, including those describing molecular systems [1–6], ultracold atoms [7, 8], and lattice elec-tronic systems such as twisted bilayer graphene [9]. When the particle number is fixed, the canonical ensemble provides the standard framework for calculating thermal properties: the partition function and observables are evaluated within the selected fixed-particle-number subspace. Classical canon-ical methods have been developed [10–13], among which Lanczos-based approaches [12] provide rigorous complexity guarantees under general parameter regimes. However, their computational cost scales linearly with the dimension of the selected sector in general, which grows exponentially with system size. In contrast to classical algorithms, a rigorous quantum framework for estimating canonical ther-mal properties within a fixed-particle-number sector has not yet been established. For example, sev-eral algorithms have been introduced within the framework of Lindbladians [4, 14–17], but the few designed for fermionic systems are not directly applicable to estimating canonical thermal proper-ties. In particular, the Lindbladian constructions in [15, 17] do not preserve particle-number symme-try. Although a number-preserving Lindbladian was proposed in [4], their theoretical guarantees are restricted to estimating ground-state properties of Hartree-Fock models. In addition to these Lind-bladian algorithms, algorithms based on quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) or linear combinations of unitaries (LCU) enable the estimation of grand-canonical thermal properties [18– 23]. As a result, for physically relevant models, we identify system-size-independent constant-temperature regimes in which the proposed quantum algorithms asymptotically outperform classical methods based on the Lanczos algorithm. > II. RELATED WORK

While a plenty of frameworks for many-body systems in classical algorithms are there (e.g. see [10, 11, 13, 32–34] and references therein), generally-applicable rigorous guarantees of time complexity for interacting fermionic systems are observed very scarcely. To our knowledge, the Lanczos method with stochastic trace estimation or its variants [29, 35] and randomized sampling algorithms by Tong and his colleagues [26, 31] are only the cases. The latter results are applicable for the grand canonical ensemble and shows a polynomial-time guarantee for some parameter regimes, while the former needs an exponential time scaling linearly with the Hilbert space dimension or a fixed particle subspace without restrictions on coupling strength or temperature applicable for both canonical and grand canonical ensembles. Similar to the Lanczos approach, our framework provides both canonical and grand-canonical schemes. However, the complexity of our quantum algorithms varies with the inverse temperature, whereas the computational cost of the Lanczos algorithm scales with the dimension of the full Hilbert space or the selected particle-number sector, regardless of temperature. Accordingly, there exist constant-temperature regimes in which our algorithms are more efficient than the Lanczos method in terms of time complexity up to polynomial factors, as shown in Observation 1. Previous quantum algorithms based on LCU or QSP techniques usually rely on access to a block-encoding of the Hamiltonian [18–23, 36]. However, these results do not directly produce an algorithm for the estimation of canonical thermal properties, as they did not develop an ex-plicit circuit construction of a block encoding for a fixed-particle subspace. It is similar for other previous results that use different input models, including a walk operator [37], Pauli sampling [38], and a Trotter formula [39]. Although not demonstrated in [24], combining its fixed-sector block encoding with QSVT techniques [18] and initializing it with the proposed state (Definition 1) should yield a canonical scheme. Similar to our approach (Algorithm 1), fixed-sector initialization is also employed in the LCU-based method of [25]. However, their construction does not incor-porate number-preserving Trotter circuits and, moreover, can incur at least quadratically higher circuit-sampling complexity than ours, reflecting a common drawback of incoherent LCU methods [19, 37, 38] compared with coherent QSP implementations [18, 36]. Some of the aforementioned algorithms are able to produce thermal properties for the grand canonical ensemble with rigorous guarantees. Especially for the general case where the strength of coupling interaction can be arbitrary, Lanczos approach [29, 35], a randomized classical algorithm [26], and some of QSP and LCU approaches mentioned above can be compared to our approaches (Algorithms 2 and 3). For Algorithm 3, its complexity is comparable to QSP approaches without amplitude amplifications, which is consistent with previous results [18, 36]. Meanwhile, Algorithm 2 achieves chemical-potential-independent circuit complexity and circuit sampling complexity corre-sponding to a fixed particle number. That is, the Trotter circuits in our construction (Algorithm 2) do not involve the number operator like the methods mentioned above, thereby yielding a reduction of circuit complexity for Hamiltonian simulation. While the quantum Lindbladian algorithms in [15, 17] enable the estimation of grand-canonical properties, their results focus on extreme coupling-strength regimes. 4

Remark 1. For comparison, one may consider a standard approach that applies the Trotter for-mula directly for H − μN defined in the full Hilbert space, similar to the QSVT-based Gibbs state preparation without amplitude amplification [18, 39]. As summarized in Algorithm 3, since this approach incorporates the chemical potential into the Hamiltonian simulation, its complexity (e.g. circuit sampling complexity and gate complexity) depends on the chemical potential. Thus, in prac-tical situations where one requires to search a proper value of chemical potential, Algorithm 3 must re-execute the quantum circuit for every trial value of μ. In contrast, Algorithm 2 avoids this bot-tleneck since the chemical potential is incorporated in a classical postprocessing as discussed above. This offers a significant reduction of quantum computational resources. Additionally, the circuit sampling and gate complexities of Algorithm 2 are independent of μ, unlike Algorithm 3.
