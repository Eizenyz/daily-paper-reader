# Automated Verification of Hamiltonian Simulation by Quantum Signal Processing 

MIN LAI, National Taiwan University, Taiwan 

YU-FANG CHEN, Academia Sinica, Taiwan 

SHIH-HAN HUNG, National Taiwan University, Taiwan We present, to the best of our knowledge, the first fully automated verification framework for QSP-based Hamiltonian simulation. Our framework exploits the structure of the simulation algorithm: Hamiltonians are given as weighted sums of Pauli strings, embedded into larger unitaries through block encodings, and transformed into time-evolution operators by quantum signal processing (QSP). Using a symbolic Pauli-sum semantics for ancilla-indexed circuit blocks, our verifier proves that the distinguished all-zero ancilla block is proportional to the target Hamiltonian and infers the subnormalization factor automatically. It then verifies the QSP layer by extracting the implemented polynomial transformation and certifying its approximation error. This structure-aware abstraction is essential for scalability. Composing our verified block encoding and QSP wrapper yields an estimated macro-expanded size of about 1.52 million gates. The verifier certifies the block encoding once and checks the QSP layer through its abstract interface, without expanding the oracle calls. Our implementation verifies block encodings of structured Pauli-sum Hamiltonians with up to 256 system qubits and verifies QSP Hamiltonian-simulation instances by extracting their implemented polynomials and checking the resulting approximation bounds. These results show that fully automated verification can handle common Hamiltonian-simulation circuits at substantial scale when it exploits their algorithmic structure. 

1 Introduction 

Hamiltonian simulation is one of the central algorithmic tasks in quantum computing and a leading application area where quantum computers may provide computational advantage. It underlies applications in quantum chemistry, condensed-matter physics, and quantum algorithms more broadly. The task is defined as follows: given a Hamiltonian 𝐻 and time 𝑡 , the goal is to approximate the time-evolution operator 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 to within error 𝜖 [Lloyd 1996]. Since Feynman [1982] proposed the idea of simulating quantum systems using a quantum computer and Lloyd [1996] gave the first explicit algorithm, many quantum algorithms have been given to tackle the problem. In particular, three families of quantum algorithms dominate. 

Product formulas approximate 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 by interleaving the evolutions under the individual terms of 

𝐻 = Í𝑗 𝐻 𝑗 , and their correctness rests on nested commutator bounds [Childs et al. 2021]. Linear combination of unitaries (LCU) approximates 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 as a weighted sum of easy unitaries via an ancilla-controlled selection circuit [Childs and Wiebe 2012]. Quantum signal processing (QSP) achieves nearly optimal complexity by encoding a polynomial approximation of 𝑒 −i𝑥𝑡 into the eigenphases of a block-encoding walk operator [Low and Chuang 2017, 2019]. The last two families share a common primitive: a block encoding of matrix 𝐻 , a unitary that embeds the subnormalized operator 𝐻 /𝛼 as a submatrix (in standard form, its top-left block) for some subnormalization factor 𝛼 . This allows non-unitary 𝐻 to be accessed coherently on an enlarged quantum state. A defining feature of these algorithms is that no implementation ever 

equals the target 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 : the algorithms are deliberate truncations governed by a tunable accuracy parameter, and a finite universal gate set can in any case only approximate the required rotations, as analyzed by Dawson and Nielsen [2006]. The obligation of the verification task is therefore not just exact equivalence checking, but an approximate generalization of it, i.e., deciding whether two quantum processes agree to within error 𝜖 in a suitable metric.

This is substantially harder than, and poorly served by the tools built for, its exact counterpart. The difficulty is not merely numerical. The operators are 2 𝑛 -dimensional and given only implicitly by a circuit, so approximate checking must certify a quantitative norm bound on an object it cannot represent explicitly. Furthermore, the existing techniques that make exact equivalence tractable— decision diagrams [Burgholzer and Wille 2020], stabilizer formalism [Thanos et al. 2023], path-sums [Amy 2018], weighted model-counting [Huang et al. 2026], tree automata [Chen et al. 2023a,b, 2025], and ZX-calculus rewriting [Coecke and Duncan 2011]—primarily target circuit-to-circuit comparisons or state-based properties, rather than directly certifying a spectral-norm bound against an operator-level specification without a reference circuit. 

Automated verification of Hamiltonian simulation. Our main contribution is a novel solution to automatically verifying Hamiltonian simulation based on quantum signal processing. Here, Hamiltonian simulation verification means proving that a quantum circuit generated from a Hamiltonian 𝐻 correctly approximates the intended time-evolution operator 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 .This gap is especially visible in block-encoding-based Hamiltonian simulation. In these algorithms, correctness is not a statement that the whole circuit unitary equals another circuit. The Hamiltonian is typically given symbolically as a weighted sum of Pauli strings, and the verifier must first show that an ancilla-selected block of a larger unitary is proportional to this Hamiltonian, while also recovering the subnormalization factor. A second semantic obligation arises from the simulation layer built around the certified block encoding. In QSP-based Hamiltonian simulation, the circuit repeatedly invokes the block encoding and its adjoint, interleaved with phase transformations, so that the overall circuit block-encodes a polynomial transformation of the encoded Hamiltonian. The final check is therefore an approx-imation guarantee: after accounting for the block-encoding subnormalization, the implemented polynomial must approximate 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 within the prescribed error bound. Thus, verification naturally decomposes into certifying the block encoding and certifying the QSP transformation. Fully automated verification of this kind remains largely out of reach for existing general-purpose quantum verification techniques. The difficulty is not merely circuit size, but the semantic gap between the generated circuit and the high-level specification it is meant to satisfy. A verifier must connect low-level gates to the high-level specification 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 . This requires certifying the block encoding of the symbolic Pauli-sum Hamiltonian, reasoning about ancilla-selected blocks rather than entire unitaries, and verifying that the surrounding QSP layer implements the intended polynomial approximation of time evolution. We present, to the best of our knowledge, the first fully automated verification framework for block-encoding-based Hamiltonian simulation circuits. Our verifier combines two complementary symbolic techniques. For block encodings, it constructs a branch-indexed symbolic model of the circuit by symbolic execution, where each branch operator is represented as a weighted sum of Pauli strings. This allows the verifier to inspect the induced all-zero ancilla block, prove that it is proportional to the target Hamiltonian, and infer the subnormalization factor automatically. The resulting block-encoding certificate is then used as an abstract interface for verifying the simulation layer. In this paper, we focus on QSP-based Hamiltonian simulation. For the QSP component, the verifier replaces each invocation of the certified block encoding by a four-block symbolic abstraction, symbolically executes the QSP circuit, and uses sound rewrite rules derived from unitarity to extract the implemented polynomial transformation. The extracted polynomial is then checked against the desired time-evolution function after accounting for the block-encoding subnormalization. Together, these two symbolic components validate the two main obligations in QSP-based Hamiltonian simulation: certifying the input block encoding and certifying the surrounding QSP transformation. Although QSP is our main target, the same decomposition Automated Verification of Hamiltonian Simulation by Quantum Signal Processing 3

also suggests a path toward verifying other block-encoding-based simulation methods, including LCU-based algorithms derived from truncated Taylor-series approximations. Our evaluation shows that this structure-aware approach scales to substantial Hamiltonian-simulation instances. The verifier checks block encodings of structured Pauli-sum Hamiltonians with up to 256 system qubits and hundreds of Pauli terms, without constructing the exponentially large system matrix. It also verifies QSP-based Hamiltonian-simulation circuits by extracting the implemented polynomial and certifying its approximation error. Composing the verified block encodings and QSP wrappers yields large Hamiltonian-simulation circuits whose correctness can be established without expanding the oracle calls. Our evaluation illustrates this composition and reports the resulting error bound and macro-expanded circuit size. These results demonstrate fully automated verification of common block-encoding-based Hamiltonian-simulation circuits at substantial scale.

Rather than treating the whole circuit as an unstructured unitary, the verifier follows the algorithmic structure of the construction: it first certifies the block encoding, then symbolically analyzes the QSP layer, and finally checks the extracted polynomial approximation. The phase ancilla register is tracked explicitly: each branch 𝑝 ∈ { 0, 1}𝑠 carries its own symbolic pair. The block-encoding ancilla register is tracked only through the two-component abstraction introduced above: the all-zero branch and the aggregate of all nonzero branches. The four-block abstraction is valid only for QSP circuits that preserve this two-component view of the block-encoding ancilla. Between two consecutive invocations of the certified block encoding, no gate may modify the system register directly. Moreover, operations on the block-encoding ancilla are restricted to those whose behavior depends only on whether the ancilla is in the all-zero state. For example, multi-controlled gates conditioned on the all-zero block-encoding ancilla state are supported, whereas operations that distinguish individual nonzero block-encoding ancilla states are not. Under these restrictions, the verifier never needs to distinguish individual nonzero block-encoding ancilla branches during QSP symbolic execution. For each phase-ancilla branch, it maintains only two symbolic components: the principal component corresponding to the all-zero block-encoding branch, and the aggregate non-principal component representing all remaining block-encoding branches.

Canonicalization and termination. Rules H and S1–S4 are implemented as a deterministic canoni-calization pass: the verifier flattens sums/products, moves scalars to the front, combines identical monomials, collects powers of 𝐻 , and removes zero terms. To see why this process terminates, imagine fully expanding the expression into a sum of monomials, without collecting coefficients. Distribution does not change these expanded terms. Each R step replaces affected terms by finitely many simpler terms: each new term has fewer non-principal blocks, or the same number but fewer occurrences of 𝐴, 𝐴 †, 𝐺, 𝐺 †. These counts cannot decrease indefinitely. Since each replacement produces only finitely many terms, only finitely many R steps are possible. Between R steps and after the last one, distribution finishes expanding a finite expression; it never factors sums back into products. Canonicalization also terminates and only simplifies or combines terms. Hence the whole process terminates.

9.2 Experimental Setup 

All experiments were run on a single workstation with 8 CPU cores and 16 GiB of RAM, running macOS 13.2 (Darwin 22.3.0, arm64). We use Python 3.11. Each benchmark is executed with a timeout of 1800 seconds. We report wall-clock verification time and maximum resident set size (RSS). 

Numerical accuracy. We use the Arb/Acb ball arithmetic provided by Python-FLINT at 60 decimal digits of working precision for all arithmetic computations, including coefficient propagation, polynomial manipulation, residual evaluation, and error-bound comparison. To check an inequality LHS ≤ RHS, the implementation requires the upper bound of the ball enclosing LHS to be strictly smaller than the lower bound of the ball enclosing RHS. Zero tests are exact. Numerical roundoff may therefore leave terms whose coefficients would be zero in exact arithmetic. During simplification, these powers of 𝐻 are retained rather than removed on the basis of their small coefficients. We report PASS when verification establishes the requested guarantee: the block-encoding error bound for TASK1, as in Theorem 4.2, or the full-interval QSP approximation for TASK2, as in Corollary 7.2. 10 Related Works 

Hamiltonian-simulation and block-encoding compilers. Several recent systems provide high-level abstractions for constructing or optimizing Hamiltonian-simulation and block-encoding circuits. QBlue [Li et al. 2025] develops a verified compiler for Hamiltonian simulation, with a circuit-generation pipeline centered on Trotterization-based simulation and backend-specific synthesis. In contrast, our work targets block-encoding and QSP-based simulation, and verifies an independently given circuit rather than a particular compiler pipeline. Cobble [Yuan 2026] targets quantum computational linear algebra and provides a high-level language for programming with block-encoded matrices. It compiles mathematical expressions over block encodings into optimized quantum circuits. SimuQ [Peng et al. 2024] provides a framework for programming Hamiltonian simulation with analog compilation. Paulihedral [Li et al. 2022], 2QAN [Lao and Browne 2022], and MarQSim [Cao et al. 2025] develop compilation and optimization techniques for Hamiltonian-simulation workloads, often targeting Pauli-string-level representations and hardware-efficient implementations. These systems are complementary to ours. They focus on generating correct or efficient circuits from high-level descriptions. Our work instead addresses compiler-independent verification: given a Hamiltonian specification and an independently provided circuit, possibly produced by a compiler, optimizer, or manual construction, our verifier checks whether the circuit satisfies the intended block-encoding and QSP-level specification. 

General-purpose quantum program verification. Quantum Hoare logic and its variants provide expressive proof systems for reasoning about quantum programs [Feng and Ying 2021; Liu et al. 2019; Unruh 2019; Ying 2012; Zhou et al. 2019]. These approaches can specify rich semantic properties using quantum predicates, often represented by Hermitian operators or projections. Approximate correctness has also been studied in Hoare-style frameworks, such as [Yan et al. 2024], which reasons about approximate relational properties between two quantum programs. However, they usually require substantial user guidance and are commonly implemented in interactive theorem provers such as Coq or Isabelle, or in deductive frameworks that still require manual proof effort. Existing automated circuit-analysis tools include symbolic verifiers based on automata or SMT [Abdulla et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2023a,b, 2025, 2023c] and equivalence checkers based on ZX-calculus, path-sums, decision diagrams, or stabilizer methods [Amy 2018; Burgholzer and Wille 2020; Chen et al. 2022; Coecke and Duncan 2011; Thanos et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2022]. These tools typically verify state-based input-output properties or compare the implementation against another circuit. Our setting is different: the specification is an operator-level Hamiltonian, and the verifier checks that the given circuit satisfies the required block-encoding and QSP cor-rectness conditions and approximates the target evolution 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 within a prescribed error bound, without requiring a reference circuit for 𝑒 −i𝐻𝑡 .
