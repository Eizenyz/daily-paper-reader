## Hardware-Friendly Estimation of LCU-Based Expectation Values via Quantum-to-Classical Workload Shifting 

Hari Hara Suthan Chittoor 1, ∗ and Thirumalai Manimaran 1, †

> 1

QpiAI India Private Limited, Bengaluru, India – 560065 

Estimating the expectation value of an observable O with respect to a quantum state evolved under a general, possibly non-unitary, operator A is a fundamental task in quantum simulation, quantum chemistry, and variational quantum algorithms. Existing approaches based on the linear combina-tion of unitaries (LCU) framework implement A on quantum hardware, requiring ancilla qubits, controlled unitaries and, in randomized variants, a fresh set of circuit executions for every Monte Carlo sample. These requirements are costly on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) and early fault-tolerant devices. We propose HERMES: Hardware-Efficient Reusable Measurements for Estimating LCU-based expectation value S, which offloads the quantum processing of A entirely to the classical domain. By expanding both A and O in the Pauli basis and replacing the quantum evaluation of A with classical Monte Carlo sampling over pairs of Pauli terms, HERMES rewrites 

μ = Tr( OAρA †)/Tr( AρA †) as a ratio of weighted sums of Pauli expectation values on the unmod-ified state ρ, requiring no ancilla qubits, controlled unitaries, or quantum implementation of A.A two-level measurement reduction scheme, combining Pauli deduplication and commuting-group partitioning, bounds the number of distinct measurement settings independently of the number of Monte Carlo samples, unlike randomized LCU methods such as single-ancilla LCU, whose circuit executions grow linearly with the sample count. We demonstrate HERMES on ground-state energy estimation of the H2 and LiH molecules via imaginary-time evolution, benchmarked against Pauli single-ancilla LCU baseline. HERMES achieves accuracy comparable to or better than the base-line with shallow circuits that, under qubit-wise commuting (QWC) grouping, contain no two-qubit gates beyond state preparation. It also shows greater robustness to hardware noise and completes the noisy LiH simulation that was infeasible for the baseline, which makes it attractive for NISQ devices, albeit with a classical overhead that grows with the number of Pauli terms in A.

I. INTRODUCTION 

Quantum computing holds transformative promise for simulating quantum systems, solving linear systems, pro-cessing quantum information, and performing optimiza-tion tasks intractable for classical computers [1–4]. Acentral computational primitive in many of these appli-cations is estimating expectation values of the form 

μ = Tr( OAρA †)Tr( AρA †) ,

where ρ is a quantum state, O is a Hermitian observable, and A is a general possibly non-unitary operator. This setting covers Hamiltonian simulation ( A = e−iHt ) [5, 6], ground-state estimation via imaginary-time evolution (A = e−τ H ) [7–9], and quantum linear systems ( A ∝

B−1) [10]. In all cases, the desired output is the scalar 

μ—not the full evolved state—a distinction central to this work. The standard approach implements A via the lin-ear combination of unitaries (LCU) method [11], which requires O(log M ) ancilla qubits and multi-qubit con-trolled gates. LCU is the foundational primitive for block encodings and quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) [12], a framework of remarkable generality: by

applying a polynomial transformation to the singular val-ues of a block-encoded operator, QSVT subsumes quan-tum search, phase estimation, Hamiltonian simulation, and quantum linear system solvers [13] — thereby pro-viding a unification of most known quantum algorithms, including Grover’s search [14] and Shor’s factoring [15]. However, the ancilla register and multi-qubit controlled gate overhead of LCU place it out of reach on current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) [16, 17] and early fault-tolerant [18, 19] hardware. To reduce circuit complexity, [20] introduced single-ancilla LCU , which eliminates the multi-qubit ancilla register by randomizing over pairs of LCU terms and executing independent Hadamard-test circuits, each re-quiring only a single ancilla qubit. This shifts part of the workload from quantum to classical processing — not to reduce total computational cost, but to make the quantum portion executable on current hardware where deep multi-qubit circuits are infeasible — at the cost of increased total circuit executions. The trade-offs across the full coherent-to-randomized spectrum were studied in [21]. Stochastic combination of unitaries was proposed in [22] to reduce per-sample circuit depth. [23] extended randomized LCU to effective states of the form U ρV †.Nevertheless, all randomized variants share a common requirement: the quantum hardware must coherently im-plement at least some unitaries in the LCU of A, and each Monte Carlo sample demands a fresh circuit execution. Consequently, for a fixed number of shots per sample, the total number of quantum circuit executions grows arXiv:2610.32841v1 [quant-ph] 23 Oct 2026 2linearly with the sample count, and no measurements can be reused across samples. A complementary and independently developed line of work addresses the measurement side of the problem. For an observable O with L Pauli terms, naive expectation estimation of O requires up to L distinct measurement settings. Pauli grouping methods reduce this overhead by partitioning the Pauli strings of O into groups that can be measured simultaneously. Qubit-wise commuting (QWC) groups share a common tensor-product eigenba-sis and therefore require only single-qubit rotations, with the minimization of the number of groups being cast as a minimum clique cover problem that is solved heuristi-cally [24]. Partitioning into general mutually commut-ing groups reduces the number of measurement settings further, since each group can be simultaneously diago-nalized by a Clifford circuit, at the cost of two-qubit gates in the rotation circuit [25]; scalable partitioning algorithms exist for both QWC and general commut-ing groups [26]. A distinct strategy, unitary partition-ing, instead reduces the number of terms to be estimated by appending additional coherent rotations to the state-preparation circuit [27]. However, this line of work has been developed primarily for estimating the expectation value of a fixed observable, and has not been integrated with LCU-based frameworks for estimating μ — where the measurement structure is richer and depends jointly on the Pauli strings of both A and O.Despite the significant progress in both directions, a gap remains: to the best of our knowledge, no existing approach avoids ancilla qubits, controlled unitary gates, and any quantum implementation of A, thereby keep-ing the quantum circuits shallow and free of multi-qubit gates beyond state preparation. This gap is of practi-cal importance, since on current NISQ hardware the fi-delity of an estimate is limited primarily by circuit depth and by the number of two-qubit gates, whose error rates are substantially higher than those of single-qubit oper-ations. Crucially, however, not every shift of workload from quantum to classical processing is beneficial: clas-sical simulation of generic quantum states and circuits is believed to require resources that grow exponentially with the number of qubits [1, 16]. The key is to offload only those tasks that remain classically tractable, such as sampling over the Pauli expansion of A when it con-tains a manageable number of terms, while retaining the quantum hardware for estimating the Pauli expectation values of ρ.In this work, we present HERMES (Hardware-

Efficient Reusable Measurements for Estimating LCU-based expectation value S), an algorithm that takes a fun-damentally different approach by entirely offloading the quantum processing associated with A to the classical domain. By expanding both A and O in the Pauli basis and replacing the direct quantum implementation of A

with classical Monte Carlo sampling over pairs of Pauli terms, drawn with probabilities proportional to the mag-nitudes of the corresponding coefficients of A, HERMES rewrites μ as a ratio of weighted sums of Pauli expecta-tion values {Tr( P ρ )} evaluated on the unmodified input state ρ. The quantum hardware is never asked to im-plement A: its only tasks are state preparation, Clifford basis rotations for simultaneous measurement of groups of Pauli strings, and computational-basis measurement, with no ancilla qubits. This workload shift is classically tractable whenever A has a manageable number of terms in the Pauli basis, as is the case for low-degree poly-nomial approximations of local Hamiltonians. Since μ

is expressed entirely in terms of expectation values of Pauli strings that arise as products of strings from A

and O, the number of distinct Pauli expectation values, and hence of measurement settings, is bounded above independently of the Monte Carlo sample count. A two-level measurement reduction scheme — Pauli deduplica-tion followed by commuting-group partitioning — fur-ther reduces the number of measurement settings, which therefore saturates as the sample count grows, whereas in single-ancilla LCU every sample requires a fresh circuit execution. With QWC grouping, two-qubit gates can be avoided entirely beyond state preparation, making HER-MES particularly hardware-friendly. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews the LCU framework, formulates the expectation value estimation problem, and describes the single-ancilla LCU method that serves as the baseline. Section III presents the proposed HERMES algorithm, including its classical preprocessing, quantum process-ing, and classical post-processing stages. Section IV re-ports numerical simulation results for ground-state en-ergy estimation of the H2 and LiH molecules, bench-marked against the Pauli single-ancilla LCU (PSA-LCU) method. Section V concludes the paper and discusses the applicability of HERMES to other LCU-based problems together with open research directions.

The product of two Pauli strings is obtained by multiplying the corresponding single-qubit operators independently at each qubit, resulting in another Pauli string up to an overall phase factor of {± 1, ±i} inherited from the single-qubit multiplication rules.  

> 2. Commuting Pauli Operators

Two Pauli strings Pi and Pj either commute or anti-commute, 

PiPj = ±Pj Pi.

They commute if the number of qubit positions at which the corresponding non-identity Pauli operators anticom-mute is even, and anticommute otherwise. Equivalently, 

[Pi, P j ] = PiPj − Pj Pi = 0 

for commuting pairs, while 

PiPj + Pj Pi = 0 

for anticommuting pairs. The commutation property is fundamental to measure-ment optimization in quantum algorithms [26]. Since mutually commuting Pauli operators share a common eigenbasis, they can be measured simultaneously after an appropriate basis transformation. Consequently, com-muting Pauli strings can be grouped into measurement sets, allowing multiple expectation values to be estimated from the outcomes of a single measurement setting and thereby reducing the total measurement cost.

Following classical preprocessing, the HERMES algo-rithm proceeds to the quantum processing stage. The purpose of this stage is to estimate the expectation val-ues of all distinct Pauli observables contained in Punique 

using a finite number of quantum measurement configu-rations. For each commuting group Pg where g ∈ [G], the algo-rithm first computes a unitary transformation Vg that si-multaneously diagonalizes all Pauli operators within the group Pg . Since all operators commute, such a com-mon eigenbasis always exists. The quantum state ρ is then prepared on quantum hardware, after which the basis-rotation unitary Vg is applied. The rotated state is subsequently measured in the computational basis us-ing a finite number of shots. From the resulting bit-string statistics, the expectation values of all Pauli ob-servables belonging to that commuting group are simul-taneously estimated through classical processing. Conse-quently, a single measurement setting yields information about multiple observables simultaneously, substantially reducing the quantum execution cost. Moreover, if qubit-wise commuting (QWC) grouping is used, at the second level of measurement reduction in classical preprocess-ing stage, the basis rotations Vg consist solely of single-qubit Clifford gates, so no two-qubit entangling gates are needed beyond those used for state preparation. The complete quantum processing stage is summarized in Al-gorithm 2 and is depicted in Fig. 1c.

Overall, HERMES transforms the estimation of non-unitary expectation values into a classical-quantum coop-erative estimation problem, where the quantum hardware 8is responsible only for measuring Pauli observables on the unmodified state ρ and all structure associated with A

is handled classically. By combining Monte Carlo sam-pling, Pauli deduplication, commuting-group partition-ing, and cached reconstruction of repeated sample contri-butions, HERMES substantially reduces both the num-ber of distinct quantum measurement settings and the classical postprocessing overhead. The method requires no ancilla qubits, no controlled unitary gates (with QWC grouping), no amplitude amplification, and no quantum evaluation of A in any form, reducing the quantum cir-cuit to state preparation and single-qubit Clifford ba-sis rotations — making HERMES directly executable on near-term devices where qubit count, circuit depth, and measurement cost are the dominant practical constraints.   

> IV. RESULTS A. Benchmark and simulation setup

The performance of HERMES , with QWC grouping, is assessed through numerical simulations of ground-state energy estimation for the H2 and LiH molecules, each evaluated at five bond lengths so that the behavior of the algorithm can be examined across the potential en-ergy curve. Both molecules are described in the STO-3G basis. The qubit Hamiltonians contain 15 Pauli terms on 4 qubits for H2 and 631 Pauli terms on 12 qubits for LiH , independent of the bond length. The molecu-lar Hamiltonians and the full configuration interaction (FCI) energies EF CI are obtained from the PennyLane quantum chemistry dataset [33]. The results are benchmarked against the Pauli single-ancilla LCU (PSA-LCU) method, a special case of the single-ancilla LCU framework [20] in which every uni-tary in the LCU decomposition of A is a Pauli string. Both methods therefore operate on an identical Pauli expansion of A, so that differences in performance can be attributed to the estimation strategy rather than to the decomposition. Implementing PSA-LCU requires one ancilla qubit in addition to the system register and controlled Pauli (two-qubit) gates, whereas HERMES 

uses neither: its circuits consist of state preparation and single-qubit basis rotations only.

> V. CONCLUSIONS

We have presented HERMES , a hybrid classical–quantum algorithm for estimating 

μ = Tr( OAρA †)/Tr( AρA †) that shifts the entire quantum processing of A to the classical domain. By expanding A and O in the Pauli basis and sampling from the coefficient distribution of A, HERMES rewrites μ

as a ratio of weighted sums of Pauli expectation values on the unmodified input state ρ. The resulting quantum circuit consists solely of state preparation and Clifford basis rotations, with no ancilla qubits, no controlled unitaries (with QWC grouping) and no quantum im-plementation of A. A two level measurement reduction scheme bounds the number of distinct measurement settings independently of the Monte Carlo sample count, in contrast to existing randomized LCU methods such as single-ancilla LCU, whose circuit executions grow linearly with the sample count. Simulation results showed that, for ground-state estimation of H2 and LiH ,

HERMES (with QWC grouping) achieves comparable or improved accuracy relative to PSA-LCU baseline with circuits that contain no two-qubit gates and have a much smaller depth. Furthermore, HERMES exhibits greater robustness to hardware noise and remains computationally feasible for LiH . These properties make 

HERMES attractive for NISQ and early fault-tolerant devices, where ancilla qubits and multi-qubit gates are costly, although this comes with a classical overhead that grows with the number of Pauli terms in A.In addition to ground-state estimation, HERMES is applicable to other LCU-based expectation value prob-lems, such as Hamiltonian simulation [6] and quantum linear systems [10], provided that O and A, or a poly-nomial approximation of A, admit Pauli expansions with a manageable number of terms; the same sampling and measurement pipeline then applies. A key open research direction is the derivation of a rigorous convergence guar-antee for the HERMES estimator, providing explicit sample-complexity bounds. Exploring general commut-ing groups could further reduce the number of measure-ment settings, at the cost of additional two-qubit gates in the basis-rotation circuits. Moreover, since Pauli par-titioning is part of the classical preprocessing, advances in Pauli-partitioning algorithms, such as the method pro-posed in [26], would directly benefit HERMES .
