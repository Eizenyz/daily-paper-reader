Abstract 

One of the main challenges in quantum simulation is the prohibitive cost of computing its solutions in the semi-classical regime, in which the de Broglie wavelength is small compared with the characteristic length scale and the solution is highly oscillatory. This difficulty is overcome by using the Weyl variable, under which the solution is not oscillatory. Furthermore, we use the exact Hermite moments and quantum singular value transformation to treat the polynomial and Fourier components of the potential, resulting in a quantum algorithm efficient for all ranges of wavelengths. Specifically, it has a polynomial complexity in spatial dimension, and discretization and query bounds 

without negative powers of possibly small wavelength, thus enabling it to capture the correct physical observables even if the spatial grid does not resolve the frequency, hence defying the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. 

Introduction. Consider the von Neumann equation for mixed-state quantum dynamics: iℏ∂t̂ρ = [ ̂ H, ̂ρ],̂ H = − ℏ2

2m ∆ + V (X), (1) where V : Rd → R is a real-valued potential, [ ̂ H, ̂ρ ] = ̂

Ĥρ−̂ρ̂ H is the commutator, and ̂ρ (t) is a density operator with integral kernel ρ(t; X, Y ), X, Y ∈ Rd. We first nondimensionalize it by choosing a characteristic length 

L (e.g., the scale over which V varies) and a characteristic energy E0 (e.g., the typical kinetic or potential energy). Rescaling space, time, and energy by L, L√m/E 0, and 

E0, respectively, gives the dimensionless von Neumann equation (for clarity, we use the same variables) iε∂ t̂ρ ε = [ ̂ Hε,̂ρ ε],̂ Hε = −(ε2/2)∆ + V (X), (2) where ε = ℏ/(L√mE 0) is the ratio of the reduced de Broglie wavelength to the characteristic length scale L.The case of ε ≪ 1 is referred to as the semiclassical regime. Such a scaling also arises in the Born–Oppenheimer ap-proximation [1], in which ε ≪ 1 is proportional to the square root of the electron-to-nuclear mass ratio. Two main challenges in the numerical simulation of quantum dynamics are the curse of dimensionality, since 

d can be O(10)– O(10 2), which motivates quantum com-putation, and the highly oscillatory nature of the solution, with frequency O(ε−1), in the semiclassical regime ε ≪ 1. In the latter case, the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theo-rem requires several mesh points per wavelength to resolve these oscillations, leading to a spatial mesh h = O(ε).

This demands O(ε−d) spatial unknowns for a wave func-tion and O(ε−2d) entries for a dense representation of the density-matrix kernel. A time integrator that resolves the quantum state may also require O(ε−1) time steps over an O(1) time interval. There has been tremendous effort devoted to overcom-ing the second computational challenge. For the linear Schrödinger equation describing pure-state quantum dy-namics, the classical Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) analysis gives an asymptotic solution whose phase and amplitude are governed by the eikonal and transport equa-tions, respectively. However, this description is no longer valid after the formation of caustics [2–4]. The more accurate Gaussian wave-packet methods can relax the numerical constraints to h = O(√ε) [5–7], yet they still have a computational complexity of O(ε−d/ 2) per time step, and are primarily designed for the regime ε ≪ 1, with accuracy guarantees that do not generally extend uniformly to ε = O(1). Previously, it was demonstrated that, for the linear Schrödinger equation, time-splitting (or Trotter splitting) schemes allow the time step τ to be independent of ε when physical observables, rather than the wave function itself, are required to be accurate [8–12]. However, for quantum dynamics, the more com-putationally challenging issue is spatial resolution. Even with a highly accurate spectral method, one still needs 

h = O(ε). Breaking this prohibitive spatial-resolution constraint, in addition to the curse of dimensionality, is the aim of the present work. We introduce a quantum algorithm for the von Neu-mann equation (2) that, in addition to addressing the curse of dimensionality, achieves the following goals: (i) in the regime ε ≪ 1, the spatial mesh size h can be 1arXiv:2609.07384v1 [quant-ph] 7 Sep 2026 chosen independently of ε when computing prescribed smooth physical observables; and (ii) the algorithm works uniformly for all ε, and hence is applicable in both the semiclassical and quantum regimes. Moreover, we con-struct an end-to-end quantum algorithm for this method and prove, under the regularity and structured access assumptions specified below, that its resource bounds contain no negative power of ε and depend polynomially 

on the spatial dimension d. To our knowledge, this is the first end-to-end quantum algorithm with an ε-uniform 

representation, allowing Hamiltonian simulation and ob-servable readout over the full range of ε. In particular, when computing physical observables, the method does not require Nyquist–Shannon resolution of the original quantum state, because it does not need to computation-ally resolve the ε-scale oscillations when ε ≪ 1. Such an algorithm is important for applications ranging from Born–Oppenheimer molecular dynamics and nonadia-batic surface hopping in photochemistry [13–15], proton transfer in enzymes and in solution [16, 17], to mixed quantum–classical dynamics and strong-field ionization [18, 19]. A main ingredient of our method is the use of Weyl vari-ables , in which the solution is nonoscillatory, a property proved by Filbet and Golse [20, 21]. This property moti-vated their construction of a classical Hermite method with ε-uniform approximation estimates. In their one-dimensional classical method, the full projected potential term generally produces dense coupling matrices among the Hermite modes, so its application has a cost quadratic 

in the number of retained modes at every spatial grid point. Their more efficient Taylor approximation replaces these dense matrices by fixed-bandwidth couplings, but is valid only for ε ≪ 1, not for ε = O(1). We overcome this restriction by using an integral representation of the full symmetric potential difference, which avoids any Taylor truncation in ε and remains accurate uniformly 

for 0 < ε ≤ 1. For the polynomial component of the potential, we construct the corresponding block encoding using exact 

projected Hermite moments, which remain sparse for fixed polynomial degree. For each Fourier mode, the full symmetric difference is represented as a matrix sinc function of a sparse Hermite coordinate matrix. We use quantum singular value transformation (QSVT) in an enlarged Hermite basis to implement this generally dense matrix function without forming it explicitly, and then project back to the retained modes and combine the Fourier modes using a linear combination of unitaries (LCU) [22, 23]. Hamiltonian simulation followed by over-lap estimation returns the prescribed physical observables 

without reconstructing the full density matrix. Thus, for the structured problem class considered in this work, the quantum algorithm avoids the exponential storage cost of a direct classical discretization on the full tensor prod-uct grid when estimating a fixed number of prescribed observables.

For the mixed states and smooth observ-ables covered by Theorem 1, the algorithm achieves the prescribed observable accuracy for all 0 < ε ≤ 1 us-ing discretization parameters that do not depend on 

ε. The error bound includes discretization, quantum simulation, and readout errors. A direct tensor grid stores ( M K )d coefficients; their amplitude encoding uses 

d⌈log 2 M ⌉ + d⌈log 2 K⌉ system qubits. Under the stated regularity and structured-access assumptions, query and gate costs are polynomial in d and contain no inverse powers of ε. This advantage concerns estimation of a fixed number of smooth observables, with input prepara-tion and output normalization included in the resource accounting.
